The Kepler Problem: Planetary Orbits

From a falling apple to the orbit of Mars: Newton's law of gravity says \( \vec F = -GMm/r^2\,\hat r \), and the corresponding potential is \( U(\rho) = -k/\rho \) with \( k = GMm \) (or \( k = Gm_1 m_2 \) for the full two-body problem with reduced mass μ). This is the famous Kepler problem. We have all the machinery to solve it analytically: § 8.4 told us to plot the effective potential and read off bound vs unbound; this section turns the formal trajectory integral into a closed-form orbit shape \( \rho(\phi) \). The answer is a conic section with the central body at one focus — and from it, Kepler's three empirical laws fall out one by one.

💡
Learning Objectives:
  • Set up the Kepler trajectory integral and apply the substitution \( u = 1/\rho \) to obtain a closed-form orbit
  • Read the conic-section formula \( \rho(\phi) = p/(1 + e\cos\phi) \) and identify the semi-latus rectum \( p \) and eccentricity \( e \) in terms of physical constants of motion
  • Map energy E to orbit type: \( E = E_{\min} \) ↔ circle, \( E_{\min} < E < 0 \) ↔ ellipse, \( E = 0 \) ↔ parabola, \( E > 0 \) ↔ hyperbola
  • Derive Kepler's third law \( T^2 = 4\pi^2 \mu a^3/k \) by integrating the area-sweep equation over one orbital period
  • Recognize that Kepler's 1st law (closed conics) is special to the \( -1/\rho \) potential — Bertrand's theorem and Mercury's perihelion precession

From Trajectory Integral to Conic Section

Section 8.4 left us with a formal trajectory integral. With \( U(\rho) = -k/\rho \),

$$ \phi = \int \frac{d\rho}{\rho^2\sqrt{\dfrac{2\mu}{\ell^2}\!\left(E - \dfrac{\ell^2}{2\mu\rho^2} + \dfrac{k}{\rho}\right)}} $$

The integrand looks fearsome until you make the substitution \( u = 1/\rho \), so \( du = -d\rho/\rho^2 \). Then \( d\rho/\rho^2 = -du \), and the expression inside the square root becomes a quadratic in u:

$$ \phi = -\int \frac{du}{\sqrt{\dfrac{2\mu E}{\ell^2} + \dfrac{2\mu k}{\ell^2}\,u - u^2}} $$

The integrand is \( -du/\sqrt{C + Bu - u^2} \), the canonical form for an inverse-cosine integral. Completing the square:

$$ C + Bu - u^2 = -\left(u - \tfrac{B}{2}\right)^2 + \left(C + \tfrac{B^2}{4}\right) $$

and the integral evaluates (up to a phase choice) to \( \phi = \arccos\left[(u - u_0)/A\right] \), where \( u_0 = B/2 = \mu k/\ell^2 \) and \( A = \sqrt{C + B^2/4} = \sqrt{\mu^2 k^2/\ell^4 + 2\mu E/\ell^2} \).

Inverting and rewriting in terms of \( \rho = 1/u \):

Conic-section orbit (Kepler problem):

$$ \boxed{\rho(\phi) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\phi}} $$

where the semi-latus rectum and eccentricity are

$$ p = \frac{\ell^2}{\mu k}, \qquad e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2 E \ell^2}{\mu k^2}} $$
💡
Energy controls eccentricity; angular momentum controls size.

Two scalar constants of motion (E, ℓ) → an infinite-parameter family of orbits. Notice that for \( E = E_{\min} = -\mu k^2/(2\ell^2) \), the discriminant in \( e \) vanishes — \( e = 0 \) — and the orbit is a circle. As \( E \) climbs toward zero, \( e \) rises toward 1 — ever-more-elongated ellipses. At \( E = 0 \), \( e = 1 \) — a parabola, marginally unbound. For \( E > 0 \), \( e > 1 \) — a hyperbola.

The Conic Family: Circle → Ellipse → Parabola → Hyperbola

With the central body at the origin (one focus of the conic):

  • Circle (e = 0): ρ = p constant, every point at the same distance.
  • Ellipse (0 < e < 1): bound; perihelion ρ_min = p/(1+e), aphelion ρ_max = p/(1−e); semi-major axis a = (ρ_min+ρ_max)/2 = p/(1−e²).
  • Parabola (e = 1): marginally unbound; perihelion p/2; ρ → ∞ as φ → ±π.
  • Hyperbola (e > 1): unbound flyby; perihelion p/(1+e); allowed φ range bounded by \( |\cos\phi| < 1/e \), beyond which the formula gives ρ < 0 (= the other branch of the hyperbola).

Bound orbit (ellipse):

$$ a = \frac{p}{1 - e^2} = \frac{k}{2|E|}, \qquad b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2} = \frac{\ell}{\sqrt{2\mu|E|}} $$

Semi-major axis depends only on energy; semi-minor depends on both E and ℓ. Notice that orbits with the same E but different ℓ share the same semi-major axis but have different shapes — same period, different eccentricity.

Kepler's Three Laws

Kepler announced his three laws of planetary motion between 1609 and 1619 — empirical patterns extracted from Tycho Brahe's painstaking observations of Mars. Newton later derived all three from his law of gravity and his laws of motion. We have done two of them already; the third now falls out from the area-sweep equation:

  1. Kepler's 1st law: Each planet's orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. ✓ Just derived above for \( E < 0 \).
  2. Kepler's 2nd law: The line from the Sun to the planet sweeps equal areas in equal times. ✓ Proved in § 8.2 from \( \ell \) conservation: \( dA/dt = \ell/(2\mu) \) is constant.
  3. Kepler's 3rd law: The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis: \( T^2 \propto a^3 \).

The 3rd law follows from integrating the 2nd over one period. The total area enclosed by an ellipse is \( A = \pi a b \). Since \( dA/dt \) is constant, the period is \( T = A/(dA/dt) = 2\pi\mu ab/\ell \). Squaring and using \( b^2 = a^2(1-e^2) = ap \) (with \( p = \ell^2/(\mu k) \)), the angular momentum cancels:

Kepler's third law (modern form):

$$ \boxed{T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 \mu}{k}\,a^3} $$

For a planet around the Sun (\( \mu \approx m_{\text{planet}} \), \( k = G M_\odot m_{\text{planet}} \)) the planetary mass cancels:

$$ T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G M_\odot}\,a^3 $$

— the constant of proportionality is the same for every planet in the solar system. That's exactly the empirical pattern Kepler observed: T²/a³ is a universal constant.

Why Is 1/ρ So Special?

💡
Bertrand's Theorem.

For any central force, ℓ-conservation guarantees Kepler's 2nd law. But Kepler's 1st law — orbits close exactly into stationary ellipses, never precessing — is special. Bertrand's theorem (Joseph Bertrand, 1873) states that only two central potentials produce closed orbits for all bounded initial conditions:

  • The Kepler potential \( U \propto -1/\rho \) (inverse-square attractive force) — closed ellipses.
  • The 3D harmonic oscillator \( U \propto \rho^2 \) (linear restoring force) — closed ellipses centered on the origin.

Any other attractive potential — Yukawa (\( -e^{-\rho/r_0}/\rho \)), Lennard-Jones, even \( U \propto -1/\rho^{1.01} \) — produces precessing rosettes: orbits close to ellipses but with the perihelion slowly rotating. Mercury's perihelion advances by about 5600 arc-seconds per century (most due to other planets, but ~43"/century unexplained by Newton). Einstein's general relativity, which effectively modifies the gravitational potential with a small \( -1/\rho^3 \) correction, predicted exactly that extra 43"/century — one of GR's first triumphs in 1915.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Plot the four canonical Kepler orbit types using ρ(φ) = p/(1+e cos φ)
mu, k, ell = 1.0, 1.0, 1.0
p = ell**2 / (mu * k)

cases = [
    ('Circle  (e = 0)',    0.00, '#00f3ff'),
    ('Ellipse (e = 0.5)',  0.50, '#00ff88'),
    ('Parabola (e = 1)',   1.00, '#ffbe0b'),
    ('Hyperbola (e = 1.5)', 1.50, '#ff006e'),
]
fig = go.Figure()
for label, e, color in cases:
    if e < 1:           phi = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 400)
    elif e == 1:        phi = np.linspace(-np.pi*0.97, np.pi*0.97, 400)
    else:
        phi_max = np.arccos(-1/e) * 0.97
        phi     = np.linspace(-phi_max, phi_max, 400)
    rho = p / (1 + e * np.cos(phi))
    x, y = rho*np.cos(phi), rho*np.sin(phi)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=y, mode='lines',
        line=dict(color=color, width=2), name=label))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0], y=[0], mode='markers',
    marker=dict(color='#ffbe0b', size=12, symbol='star'), name='Sun (focus)'))

    yaxis=dict(range=[-5, 5], zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    title='The Kepler conic family',
    height=500)
fig.show()

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Compare Kepler (closes) vs Yukawa (precesses): RK4 in Cartesian.
def make_F(potential):
    def F(r):
        rmag = np.sqrt(r @ r)
        if potential == 'kepler':   return -r / rmag**3
        if potential == 'yukawa':   # U = -exp(-r/μ)/r, with μ = 5
            mu_y = 5
            return -r * np.exp(-rmag/mu_y) * (1/rmag**3 + 1/(mu_y*rmag**2))
    return F

def rk4(F, s, h, N):
    def rhs(s): return np.concatenate([s[2:], F(s[:2])])
    out = np.zeros((N, 4)); s = s.copy()
    for i in range(N):
        out[i] = s
        k1 = rhs(s); k2 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k1)
        k3 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k2); k4 = rhs(s + h*k3)
        s = s + (h/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)
    return out

s0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.85])
trK = rk4(make_F('kepler'), s0, 0.02, 3000)
trY = rk4(make_F('yukawa'), s0, 0.02, 3000)

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=trK[:,0], y=trK[:,1], mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=1.5), name='Kepler (closes)'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=trY[:,0], y=trY[:,1], mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#ff006e', width=1.5), name='Yukawa (precesses)'))
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(range=[-3, 3], zerolinecolor='#808080', scaleanchor='y'),
    yaxis=dict(range=[-3, 3], zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    title='Bertrand: only 1/ρ closes; everything else precesses',
    height=500)
fig.show()
