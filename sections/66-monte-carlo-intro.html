Why Use Randomness?

Everything so far has been deterministic: given the same inputs, you get the same outputs. Now we flip that idea on its head. Monte Carlo methods use randomness to solve problems that are perfectly deterministic — like computing areas, volumes, and integrals. The idea is simple and profound: throw darts randomly, count the hits, and the fraction that lands inside tells you the answer.

Learning Objectives
  • Understand the Monte Carlo philosophy: random sampling as a computational tool for deterministic problems
  • Estimate π by throwing random “darts” at a circle inscribed in a square
  • See why Monte Carlo wins in high dimensions: the curse of dimensionality kills grid methods
  • Watch the law of large numbers in action: more samples → better estimate

The Monte Carlo Idea:

$$ \text{Quantity} \approx (\text{Total volume}) \times \frac{\text{Hits inside}}{\text{Total samples}} $$

Throw \( N \) random points uniformly into a known region. Count how many land inside the target. The fraction \( N_{\text{in}} / N \) estimates the ratio of areas (or volumes). As \( N \to \infty \), the estimate converges to the true value.

Why “Monte Carlo”?

Named after the famous casino in Monaco. Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann developed these methods at Los Alamos in the 1940s while working on nuclear weapons simulations. The name was a code word — the calculations relied on random numbers, just like casino games. Today, Monte Carlo methods are used everywhere: physics, finance, machine learning, and drug design.

After throwing 100 darts, your estimate of π is noisy (maybe 3.0 or 3.3). After 10,000 darts, it’s much closer to 3.14159. Why?
Explanation: The law of large numbers guarantees that as \( N \to \infty \), the sample average converges to the true average. With 100 darts, random fluctuations are significant. With 10,000 darts, they average out, and the estimate stabilizes near π. Each dart is equally “accurate” — it’s the averaging that improves.
Throw 5,000 darts several times. Typically, how far is the estimate from true π? \( |\hat{\pi} - \pi| \approx \) (watch the Error readout across runs)
Explanation: Each run lands somewhere different — that’s randomness — but the typical miss is remarkably consistent: around 0.02. The dart count inside the circle is binomial, so the estimate’s spread is \( 4\sqrt{p(1-p)}/\sqrt{N} \approx 0.023 \) for \( N = 5{,}000 \). The key insight: individual estimates are random, but the size of the error is predictable — and shrinks like \( 1/\sqrt{N} \).
If you reset and throw again, will you get the exact same estimate of π?
Explanation: Each run uses different random points, so you get a different estimate. This is the fundamental nature of Monte Carlo: the answer is a random variable. But while individual runs differ, their average converges to the true value, and the spread shrinks as \( 1/\sqrt{N} \).
Explorer: The Curse of Dimensionality

To estimate a volume with a regular grid, you need \( n^d \) points (\( n \) per dimension, \( d \) dimensions). In 2D: 100 points for a 10×10 grid. In 10D: \( 10^{10} = \) 10 billion points! Monte Carlo doesn’t care about dimension — and real problems live in exactly this regime: pricing an option on 100 correlated assets, configuration integrals over thousands of particle coordinates, Bayesian posteriors over dozens of parameters. 1,000 random points work in any dimension.

With \( n = 10 \) points per axis, how many total grid points are needed in 6 dimensions?
Explanation: A grid with \( n \) points per axis in \( d \) dimensions has \( n^d \) total points. With \( n = 10, d = 6 \): \( 10^6 = 1{,}000{,}000 \). This grows exponentially with dimension. By dimension 10, you need \( 10^{10} \) = 10 billion points — completely impractical.
A 10-points-per-axis grid in 6 dimensions uses \( 10^6 \) points. If 5,000 MC samples give similar accuracy, MC uses times fewer points.
Explanation: The grid needs \( 10^6 = 1{,}000{,}000 \) points; MC needs only 5,000. The ratio is \( 10^6 / 5{,}000 = 200 \). And this gap explodes with dimension: in 10D the grid would need \( 10^{10} \) points while MC still uses a few thousand, because its \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) convergence depends only on \( N \), not on dimension. This is Monte Carlo’s superpower.
When is Monte Carlo preferable to grid-based methods? (Select all)
Explanation:
  • 1D smooth functions: ✗ Trapezoidal or Simpson’s rule beats MC in low dimensions. MC is overkill here.
  • High dimensions: ✓ This is MC’s sweet spot. Grids are impossible but random sampling is trivial.
  • Complicated regions: ✓ For irregular boundaries (e.g., the inside of a fractal), grid methods need complex boundary detection. MC just checks “is this point inside?”
  • Machine precision: ✗ MC converges as \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) — terribly slow for high precision. You’d need \( 10^{30} \) samples for 15 digits.

The \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) Rule

Monte Carlo error decreases as \( \sigma / \sqrt{N} \) where \( \sigma \) is the standard deviation of the integrand and \( N \) is the number of samples. To halve the error, you need four times as many samples. This is slow compared to deterministic methods in low dimensions — but it’s independent of dimension. In 100 dimensions, \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) still works the same way. We’ll explore this convergence rate in detail in the next section.

Run the π estimator with 5,000 samples. Then run it again. Do the two convergence curves look identical?
Explanation: Each run uses different random numbers, so the convergence path is different. But both paths converge to the same true value. The fluctuations are different, but they both shrink as \( 1/\sqrt{N} \). This is the fundamental promise of Monte Carlo: different random journeys, same destination.
To halve the Monte Carlo error, you need to multiply the number of samples by . (Since error ∝ \( 1/\sqrt{N} \))
Explanation: Error \( \propto 1/\sqrt{N} \). To halve the error: \( 1/\sqrt{N'} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1/\sqrt{N} \), so \( \sqrt{N'} = 2\sqrt{N} \), giving \( N' = 4N \). You need 4 times as many samples. This is the price of Monte Carlo’s simplicity — slow convergence, but dimension-independent.
Select the Gaussian integral. The true value is approximately 0.886. Does Monte Carlo still converge to the correct answer?
Explanation: Monte Carlo works for any integrable function. The dart-board (hit/miss) method is just one approach. More generally, you can estimate \( \int f(x)\,dx \) by sampling random \( x \) values and averaging \( f(x) \). The Gaussian integral converges just as reliably as the π estimate.

Python 

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Estimate π by throwing darts
N = 10000
x = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, N)
y = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, N)
inside = x**2 + y**2 <= 1

# Running estimate of π
cumulative_inside = np.cumsum(inside)
n_array = np.arange(1, N + 1)
pi_estimate = 4 * cumulative_inside / n_array

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=n_array, y=pi_estimate, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=1.5),
    name='MC estimate'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=[0, N], y=[np.pi, np.pi], mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='white', width=2, dash='dash'),
    name='True π'))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='Number of samples N',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', type='log'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Estimate of π',
               range=[2.5, 3.8],
               zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    height=450, width=600)
fig.show()

print(f'Final estimate: π ≈ {pi_estimate[-1]:.5f}')
print(f'True value:     π = {np.pi:.5f}')
print(f'Error:          {abs(pi_estimate[-1] - np.pi):.5f}')

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# MC integration: ∫₀¹ 1/(1+x) dx = ln(2) ≈ 0.6931
N = 10000
x = np.random.uniform(0, 1, N)
f_vals = 1 / (1 + x)

# Running average = running integral estimate
# (interval length = 1, so ∫f dx ≈ mean(f) × 1)
running_mean = np.cumsum(f_vals) / np.arange(1, N+1)
true_val = np.log(2)

# Error bands: ± σ/√n
running_var = np.zeros(N)
for i in range(N):
    if i > 0:
        running_var[i] = np.var(f_vals[:i+1])
    else:
        running_var[i] = 0
running_err = np.sqrt(running_var / np.arange(1, N+1))

n = np.arange(1, N+1)
fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=n, y=running_mean, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2),
    name='MC estimate'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=n, y=running_mean + running_err,
    mode='lines', line=dict(width=0),
    showlegend=False))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=n, y=running_mean - running_err,
    mode='lines', line=dict(width=0),
    fill='tonexty',
    fillcolor='rgba(0,243,255,0.15)',
    name='±1σ band'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=[1, N], y=[true_val, true_val],
    mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='white', width=2, dash='dash'),
    name=f'True = ln(2) = {true_val:.4f}'))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='N', type='log',
               zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Estimate',
               range=[0.5, 0.9],
               zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    height=450, width=600)
fig.show()
