Numerical Random Walk Simulations

We have built a beautiful theory: random walks → binomial distribution → Gaussian in the continuum limit → diffusion equation. But how do we know the theory is right? The computational-thinking answer is to simulate: generate thousands of random walks, measure their statistics, and compare to the formulas we derived. If they agree, theory is validated. If they disagree, something is wrong — in the code or in the theory.

Learning Objectives
  • Generate ensembles of random walks and collect final positions
  • Build histograms and overlay theoretical predictions (binomial, Gaussian)
  • Measure \( \langle m \rangle \) and \( \langle m^2 \rangle \) from data and compare to theory
  • Understand that simulation is validation: the third pillar of computational science alongside theory and experiment

Theoretical Predictions (Unbiased 1D Walk, N Steps):

$$ \langle m \rangle = 0, \qquad \langle m^2 \rangle = N, \qquad P(m) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi N}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{m^2}{2N}\right) $$

Our simulations should reproduce all three: zero mean, variance equal to \( N \), and a Gaussian envelope.

Explorer: Ensemble Histogram vs Theory

Launch many random walks at once. Each walker takes \( N \) unbiased \( \pm 1 \) steps. Their final positions \( m \) are binned into a histogram, and we overlay the exact binomial prediction and the Gaussian approximation. As the ensemble grows, the histogram should converge to the theory.

Run with 100 walkers, then with 20,000. What happens to the histogram?
Explanation: The shape of the distribution is set by the physics (binomial/Gaussian). More walkers just give better statistics — the histogram becomes smoother and hugs the theoretical curve. This is the law of large numbers in action.
Find the N that makes the histogram width \( \sigma \approx 15 \). . Set the step slider, then read the measured width.
Explanation: The width is \( \sigma = \sqrt{N} \), so \( \sigma \approx 15 \) requires \( N = 15^2 = 225 \). Dial the step count to about 225 and the measured \( \sigma \) in the stats bar lands near 15. Because \( \sigma \) grows as \( \sqrt{N} \), widening the histogram by 50% (from 10 to 15) more than doubles the steps needed (from 100 to 225).
For large \( N \), the binomial (dots) and Gaussian (curve) overlays…
Explanation: For large \( N \) (say \( N \geq 30 \)), the binomial and Gaussian are nearly indistinguishable — this is the central limit theorem at work. The Gaussian is the continuum limit of the discrete binomial distribution (Section 7.9 will prove this via Stirling's approximation).
Explorer: Measured Moments vs Theory

From the simulated ensemble, we compute the sample mean \( \overline{m} = \frac{1}{N_w}\sum_w m_w \) and sample second moment \( \overline{m^2} = \frac{1}{N_w}\sum_w m_w^2 \), and compare to the theoretical predictions \( \langle m \rangle = 0 \) and \( \langle m^2 \rangle = N \). We sweep the number of steps and plot the agreement.

Test Your Understanding

Use the Measured Moments explorer.

With 500 walkers per point, what shape does the measured \( \langle m^2 \rangle \) vs \( N \) plot look like?
Explanation: For an unbiased walk, \( \langle m^2 \rangle = N \) — a straight line through the origin with slope 1. Diffusive spread (\( \langle m^2 \rangle \propto N \)) is slower than ballistic (\( \propto N^2 \)) but faster than random jitter (\( \propto \text{const} \)).
The measured mean \( \overline{m} \) fluctuates around zero. As you increase walkers per point, those fluctuations…
Explanation: Individual walkers have spread \( \sqrt{N} \), so the sample mean of \( N_w \) walkers has spread \( \sqrt{N}/\sqrt{N_w} \) — the standard error. More walkers → tighter agreement with the theoretical \( \langle m \rangle = 0 \). This is exactly the same \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) convergence we saw in Monte Carlo integration (Chapter 6).
Which statements about simulation-as-validation are true? (Select all)
Explanation:
  • Agreement builds confidence: ✓ Independent derivations (formula + simulation) rarely both fail the same way.
  • Disagreement = debug: ✓ Always investigate — sometimes the code is wrong, sometimes an assumption is off.
  • Don't need theory: ✗ Theory generalizes; simulation is one data point. Theory explains why; simulation shows that.
  • Simulations handle hard problems: ✓ Ising models, interacting walkers, absorbing boundaries — intractable analytically but easy numerically.
Explorer: Histogram Convergence

Watch the histogram build up walker by walker. We measure the RMS deviation between the measured histogram and the theoretical Gaussian at each stage. Agreement should improve like \( 1/\sqrt{N_w} \): this is the signature of Monte Carlo convergence.

Test Your Understanding

Use the Histogram Convergence explorer.

On the log-log RMS-error plot, the error falls like a straight line. What slope do you see?
Explanation: Monte Carlo error scales as \( 1/\sqrt{N_w} \), so on log-log axes the slope is \( -1/2 \). This is the universal Monte Carlo convergence rate — the same rate we saw for MC integration in Chapter 6. To halve the error, you need four times the walkers.
To reduce the RMS error by a factor of 10, roughly how many more walkers do you need?
Explanation: \( \text{error} \propto 1/\sqrt{N_w} \). To divide error by 10, we need \( N_w \to 100 N_w \). This slow convergence is the price of generality — but it is dimension-independent, which makes Monte Carlo irresistible in high dimensions.
Simulation matches theory to three decimals. What should you conclude?
Explanation: Agreement is strong evidence of consistency in the tested regime. Theory still wins for generality (it works for any \( N \)), but simulation confirms we have not made an algebra error and the assumptions hold in practice. Good scientists use both — and are always suspicious of extrapolation beyond what was tested.
Explorer: Biased Random Walk (p ≠ 1/2)

What happens if the coin is unfair? Let \( p \) be the probability of a \( +1 \) step. The walk now has a drift: each step contributes \( \langle \Delta m \rangle = 2p - 1 \) on average. The mean grows linearly, \( \langle m \rangle = N(2p-1) \), and the variance picks up a ballistic piece: \( \langle m^2 \rangle = N + N(N-1)(2p-1)^2 \). At \( p = 1/2 \) the ballistic term vanishes and we recover the diffusive \( \langle m^2 \rangle = N \).

Test Your Understanding

Use the Biased Walk explorer.

Set \( N = 400 \). Find the p that centers the histogram near \( m = -80 \). .
Explanation: The center is \( \langle m \rangle = N(2p-1) \), so \( 400(2p-1) = -80 \) gives \( 2p-1 = -0.2 \) and \( p = 0.4 \). A leftward bias of just 0.1 below 0.5 shifts the whole histogram to \( m = -80 \). Nudge the bias slider to 0.4 and watch the peak land there.
For large \( N \) and \( p \neq 1/2 \), the leading behavior of \( \langle m^2 \rangle \) is…
Explanation: \( \langle m^2 \rangle = N + N(N-1)(2p-1)^2 \). For large \( N \) the second term dominates: \( \langle m^2 \rangle \approx N^2(2p-1)^2 \). This is ballistic scaling — the walker covers distance proportional to \( N \), not \( \sqrt{N} \). Bias turns diffusion into directed motion.
Which are true about biased walks? (Select all)
Explanation:
  • Variance ∝ N: ✓ \( \mathrm{Var}(m) = \langle m^2 \rangle - \langle m \rangle^2 = N\,4p(1-p) \), still linear in \( N \).
  • Ballistic at p=1/2: ✗ \( (2 \times 0.5 - 1)^2 = 0 \), so the ballistic term vanishes.
  • Real examples: ✓ Drift-diffusion is everywhere — semiconductor carriers in a field, ions in an electrolyte, biased Brownian motors.
  • Smooth limit: ✓ The formulas are continuous in \( p \); set \( p = 1/2 \) and we get \( \langle m \rangle = 0, \langle m^2 \rangle = N \).
Explorer: First-Passage Time (Return to Origin)

Start an unbiased walker at the origin. How long until it returns? Each walker is run with a while loop that stops the first time it hits \( m = 0 \) again. We record the return time \( \tau \) for many walkers and histogram them. The result is genuinely surprising: in 1D, an unbiased walker is guaranteed to return (probability 1) — but the mean return time is infinite. The distribution \( P(\tau) \sim \tau^{-3/2} \) has a fat power-law tail that drags the average to infinity.

⚠️
The Mean Diverges:

Watch the right panel: the running average return time keeps climbing as you add more walkers. It does not converge. Every increase of the step cap pulls in rare walkers with huge return times that yank the mean upward. This is the signature of a distribution with infinite mean — Pólya's recurrence theorem in action.

Test Your Understanding

Use the First-Passage Time explorer.

On the log-log histogram of return times, what slope do you observe?
Explanation: The first-return distribution for the 1D simple random walk is \( P(\tau) \sim \tau^{-3/2} \) for large \( \tau \). This is exact — derivable from the reflection principle. Because \( \int \tau \cdot \tau^{-3/2} d\tau = \int \tau^{-1/2} d\tau \) diverges, the mean is infinite.
Increase the "Max steps cap" from 10,000 to 50,000. The measured mean return time…
Explanation: Raising the cap admits walkers with huge \( \tau \). Because the tail is so fat (\( \tau^{-3/2} \)), each new decade of cap adds a non-negligible contribution to the average. The "measured mean" is an artefact of the cap — the true mean is infinite.
Which statements about 1D first-passage are true? (Select all)
Explanation:
  • Return certain: ✓ Pólya proved 1D and 2D walks are recurrent (probability 1 of return); 3D walks are transient (probability ~0.66).
  • Mean = 2: ✗ The mean is infinite. The minimum return time is 2 (must take one step out and one back), but the distribution's heavy tail makes the average diverge.
  • Median finite: ✓ Despite an infinite mean, the median is just a few steps. Mean and median can disagree wildly for heavy-tailed distributions — a key statistical lesson.
  • Simulation matters: ✓ "Returns always but mean diverges" sounds paradoxical; running the experiment makes it concrete. This is the case-in-point for computational thinking.
Key Insight: Simulation as the Third Pillar

Science used to rest on two pillars: theory (pen and paper) and experiment (lab benches and telescopes). Computational thinking adds a third: simulation — a virtual laboratory where we test theory against numerical reality before committing to expensive hardware. When simulation, theory, and experiment all agree, we have very strong evidence we understand the physics.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from math import comb

N, Nw = 100, 5000
# Each walker: sum of N ±1 steps
steps = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=(Nw, N))
m = steps.sum(axis=1)

# Histogram on even m values
edges = np.arange(-N-1, N+2, 2)
hist, _ = np.histogram(m, bins=edges, density=True)
centers = 0.5*(edges[:-1] + edges[1:])

# Exact binomial and Gaussian
k = (centers + N) // 2
binom = np.array([comb(N, int(ki)) * 0.5**N / 2 for ki in k])
gauss = np.exp(-centers**2/(2*N)) / np.sqrt(2*np.pi*N)

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Bar(x=centers, y=hist, name='simulation',
                     marker_color='#00f3ff', opacity=0.6))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=centers, y=binom, mode='markers',
    marker=dict(color='#ffbe0b', size=6), name='binomial'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=centers, y=gauss, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#ff006e', width=2), name='Gaussian'))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='m', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='P(m)', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    height=440)
fig.show()

print(f"mean = {m.mean():.3f}  (theory 0)")
print(f"⟨m²⟩ = {(m**2).mean():.2f}  (theory {N})")

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

N = 100
Nws = np.logspace(1.5, 4.5, 15).astype(int)
errs = []
edges = np.arange(-N-1, N+2, 2)
centers = 0.5*(edges[:-1] + edges[1:])
gauss = np.exp(-centers**2/(2*N)) / np.sqrt(2*np.pi*N)

for Nw in Nws:
    m = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=(Nw, N)).sum(axis=1)

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

p, N, Nw = 0.55, 200, 5000
# +1 with prob p, -1 with prob 1-p
steps = np.where(np.random.rand(Nw, N) < p, 1, -1)
m = steps.sum(axis=1)

# Theory
mean_th = N * (2*p - 1)
m2_th   = N + N*(N-1)*(2*p - 1)**2
var_th  = N * 4 * p * (1 - p)

print(f"measured ⟨m⟩ = {m.mean():.2f}   theory = {mean_th:.2f}")
print(f"measured ⟨m²⟩ = {(m**2).mean():.1f}  theory = {m2_th:.1f}")
print(f"measured Var = {m.var():.1f}      theory = {var_th:.1f}")

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Histogram(x=m, nbinsx=40,
    marker_color='#00f3ff', opacity=0.7, name='simulation'))

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# First-passage time: how long until walker returns to origin?
def first_return(cap=10000):
    pos = 0
    for t in range(1, cap + 1):
        pos += np.random.choice([-1, 1])
        if pos == 0:
            return t
    return None  # didn't return within cap

Nw = 5000
taus = [first_return(cap=20000) for _ in range(Nw)]
returned = [t for t in taus if t is not None]

print(f"return rate: {len(returned)/Nw:.3f}  (Pólya: 1.0)")
print(f"median τ:    {np.median(returned):.0f}")
print(f"mean τ:      {np.mean(returned):.0f}  (theory: ∞ — depends on cap)")

# Power-law tail: P(τ) ~ τ^(-3/2)
edges = np.logspace(0, np.log10(max(returned)), 30)
hist, _ = np.histogram(returned, bins=edges, density=True)
centers = 0.5*(edges[:-1] + edges[1:])

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=centers, y=hist, mode='markers',
    marker=dict(color='#00f3ff', size=6), name='simulation'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=centers, y=0.4*centers**-1.5,
    mode='lines', line=dict(color='#ff006e', width=2, dash='dash'),
    name='τ^(-3/2)'))
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='τ', type='log', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='P(τ)', type='log', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    height=400)
fig.show()
