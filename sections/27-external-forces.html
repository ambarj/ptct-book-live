Adding External Forces

Everything we've studied so far — free oscillation, damping, energy decay — describes a system left to its own devices after an initial push. But the most interesting physics happens when you keep pushing. A child on a swing, a radio antenna, a bridge in the wind — these are all driven oscillators.

The surprise: no matter how you start a driven oscillator, it eventually "forgets" its initial conditions and settles into a rhythm dictated entirely by the external force. The transient dies; the steady state survives. And the amplitude of that steady state depends dramatically on the driving frequency.

Learning Objectives
  • Write the equation of motion for a driven, damped oscillator
  • Separate the full solution into transient (homogeneous) and steady-state (particular) parts
  • Explain why the system "forgets" initial conditions after a few decay times
  • Read an amplitude response curve and identify the resonance region

The driven oscillator equation:

$$ \ddot{x} + 2\gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\omega_d t) $$

Full solution:
\( x(t) = \underbrace{x_{\text{trans}}(t)}_{\text{dies as } e^{-\gamma t}} + \underbrace{A\cos(\omega_d t - \delta)}_{\text{persists forever}} \)

Steady-state amplitude: \( \displaystyle A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega_d)^2}} \)

Watching the Transient Die

The full solution has two parts. The transient (red, dotted) oscillates at the natural frequency and decays exponentially. The steady state (green, dashed) oscillates at the driving frequency and persists forever. The actual motion (cyan, solid) is the sum. At early times they interfere — producing beats and messy-looking motion. But after a few decay times \( \tau = 1/\gamma \), only the steady state remains.

Explorer 1: Transient Decay Animator
Key Insight

The transient is the system's "memory" of how it started. Damping erases that memory on a timescale \( \tau = 1/\gamma \). After about \( 3\tau \), the transient has decayed to \( e^{-3} \approx 5\% \) of its initial size — the system has essentially forgotten its initial conditions. Increase \( \gamma \) and the memory fades faster.

The Amplitude Response Curve

The steady-state amplitude depends on the driving frequency \( \omega_d \). Drive far from the natural frequency \( \omega_0 \), and the response is small. Drive near \( \omega_0 \), and the amplitude grows dramatically. This is the first hint of resonance — the focus of the next section.

The left panel shows the steady-state oscillation at the current \( \omega_d \), with the driving force shown for reference. The right panel shows the full amplitude response curve \( A(\omega_d) \) with a star marking your current position.

Explorer 2: Driving Frequency Explorer
Key Insight

The amplitude response curve has a characteristic shape: a sharp peak near ω0, falling off on both sides. At low driving frequencies (much slower than the natural frequency), the system easily follows the gentle push — it moves in step with the force, like slowly rocking a swing. At high frequencies (much faster than natural), the system can’t keep up and barely responds — like trying to shake a heavy object very rapidly. The sweet spot near ωd ≈ ω0 is where the pushes are perfectly timed to build up large oscillations.

Forgetting Where You Started

Perhaps the most remarkable property of the driven oscillator: it doesn't matter where you start. Four systems with wildly different initial conditions — different positions, different velocities — all converge to the same steady-state oscillation. The driving force is a tyrant: it imposes its rhythm regardless of the system's history.

The left panel shows all four trajectories converging. The right panel shows each trajectory's deviation from the steady state — the transient component — which decays exponentially with the same rate \( e^{-\gamma t} \) for all initial conditions.

Explorer 3: Initial Condition Independence
Key Insight

This “forgetting” property has practical consequences. A radio receiver locks onto a station regardless of what it was doing when you turned it on. A vibration isolator reaches its design performance no matter how you installed it. The reason: damping gradually erases the part of the motion that depends on how the system started (the transient), leaving only the part that depends on the driving force (the steady state). Eventually, only the driving force matters.

The steady-state oscillation (green dashed) has a specific frequency and amplitude. Which of the following determines the steady-state frequency?
Explanation
The steady state oscillates at the driving frequency \( \omega_d \), not the natural frequency \( \omega_0 \). The system is forced to move at the rhythm of the external force. The natural frequency \( \omega_0 \) determines the amplitude of the response (through the resonance curve), but not the frequency of the response.
If there were no damping (\( \gamma = 0 \)), would the system still forget its initial conditions?
Explanation
Without damping, the part of the motion from the initial conditions never dies away — it oscillates at the natural frequency ω0 forever. So the total motion is a mix of two frequencies (natural + driving) that produces a perpetual beating pattern. The system never “forgets” how it started. Damping is essential for the forgetting property.

Code It Yourself

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

def steady_amp(F0m, omega0, wd, gamma):
    return F0m / np.sqrt((omega0**2 - wd**2)**2 + (2*gamma*wd)**2)

def steady_phase(omega0, wd, gamma):
    return np.arctan2(2*gamma*wd, omega0**2 - wd**2)

def driven(t, x0, v0, F0m, omega0, wd, gamma):
    A = steady_amp(F0m, omega0, wd, gamma)
    delta = steady_phase(omega0, wd, gamma)
    x_ss = A * np.cos(wd*t - delta)
    # Transient (underdamped)
    wd_nat = np.sqrt(omega0**2 - gamma**2)
    xT0 = x0 - A*np.cos(delta)
    vT0 = v0 - A*wd*np.sin(delta)
    C = xT0
    D = (vT0 + gamma*xT0) / wd_nat
    x_trans = np.exp(-gamma*t) * (C*np.cos(wd_nat*t) + D*np.sin(wd_nat*t))
    return x_trans + x_ss, x_trans, x_ss

omega0, gamma, F0m = 3.0, 0.3, 2.0
t = np.linspace(0, 20, 800)

fig = go.Figure()
x_tot, x_tr, x_ss = driven(t, 3, 0, F0m, omega0, 2.5, gamma)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x_ss, name='Steady-state',
    line=dict(color='#00ff9f', dash='dash', width=1.5)))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x_tr, name='Transient',
    line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', dash='dot', width=1.5)))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x_tot, name='Total',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2.5)))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title='Driven Oscillator: Transient + Steady State',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', range=[0, 20],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='x(t)', range=[-8, 8],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

def driven(t, x0, v0, F0m, omega0, wd, gamma):
    A = F0m / np.sqrt((omega0**2 - wd**2)**2 + (2*gamma*wd)**2)
    delta = np.arctan2(2*gamma*wd, omega0**2 - wd**2)
    x_ss = A * np.cos(wd*t - delta)
    wd_nat = np.sqrt(omega0**2 - gamma**2)
    xT0 = x0 - A*np.cos(delta)
    vT0 = v0 - A*wd*np.sin(delta)
    x_tr = np.exp(-gamma*t) * (xT0*np.cos(wd_nat*t) +
        (vT0 + gamma*xT0)/wd_nat * np.sin(wd_nat*t))
    return x_tr + x_ss

omega0, gamma, wd, F0m = 3.0, 0.3, 2.0, 2.0
t = np.linspace(0, 25, 800)

ics = [(0, 0, '#00f3ff'), (5, 0, '#ff00ff'),
       (-3, 4, '#ffff00'), (0, -6, '#ff6b6b')]

fig = go.Figure()
for x0, v0, color in ics:
    x = driven(t, x0, v0, F0m, omega0, wd, gamma)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x,
        name=f'x₀={x0}, v₀={v0}', line=dict(color=color, width=2)))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title='Four ICs → One Steady State',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', range=[0, 25],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='x(t)', range=[-8, 8],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
