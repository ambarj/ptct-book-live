Escape Velocity: The Physics

Near the ground, we treat gravity as constant: \( F = -mg \). But what if you launch a projectile really high — hundreds or thousands of kilometers? Gravity weakens as you move away from Earth. The constant-\( g \) model breaks down, and we need Newton's law of gravitation.

Learning Objectives
  • See why constant \( g \) fails for high-altitude projectiles and derive the inverse-square ODE
  • Use energy conservation to find the escape velocity \( v_0 = \sqrt{2gR} \approx 11.2 \) km/s
  • Classify trajectories: bound (returns), critical (barely escapes), and unbound (escapes with speed to spare)
  • Understand why we need numerical methods: energy gives \( v(x) \) but not \( x(t) \)

Newton's Law of Gravitation:

$$ m\ddot{x} = -\frac{GMm}{x^2} $$

where \( x \) is the distance from Earth's center, \( M \) is Earth's mass, and \( G \) is the gravitational constant.

Since \( g = GM/R^2 \) at Earth's surface (radius \( R \)), we can eliminate \( G \) and \( M \):

The equation of motion:

$$ \ddot{x} = -\frac{gR^2}{x^2} $$

This is the ODE we'll eventually solve numerically (in Section 4.16). But first, let's extract as much physics as we can analytically.

The gravitational potential energy (per unit mass) at distance \( x \) from Earth's center is:
Explanation: The force is \( F = -gR^2/x^2 \). Integrating: \( U = -\int F\,dx = -gR^2/x \) (choosing \( U \to 0 \) as \( x \to \infty \)). The potential is a well that approaches zero from below — you need to add energy to climb out of it.
Why does the constant-gravity model give the wrong answer for escape velocity?
Explanation: With constant \( g \), the potential is \( U = mgh \) — it grows linearly without bound. To reach infinity would require infinite kinetic energy, so no finite launch speed would suffice. But with inverse-square gravity, the potential \( U = -gR^2/x \) asymptotes to zero. The well has a finite depth, so a finite speed can climb out. That's why escape is possible.

The Energy Approach

Here's a beautiful trick from classical mechanics. Define \( v = dx/dt \). By the chain rule:

$$ \ddot{x} = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{dv}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt} = v\frac{dv}{dx} $$

Our ODE becomes \( v\,dv/dx = -gR^2/x^2 \), which is separable.

Integrating both sides:

$$ \frac{1}{2}v^2 = \frac{gR^2}{x} + C $$

This is energy conservation: \( \tfrac{1}{2}v^2 \) is kinetic energy per unit mass, and \( -gR^2/x \) is gravitational potential energy per unit mass.

Launching from the surface (\( x = R \)) with speed \( v_0 \) gives \( C = \tfrac{1}{2}v_0^2 - gR \), so:

Velocity as a function of position:

$$ v^2 = v_0^2 - 2gR\left(1 - \frac{R}{x}\right) $$

The projectile turns around when \( v^2 = 0 \). To never turn around (escape to infinity), we need \( v^2 \geq 0 \) as \( x \to \infty \):

$$ v_0^2 \geq 2gR \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad v_{\text{escape}} = \sqrt{2gR} $$
The Number

For Earth: \( g = 9.8 \) m/s², \( R = 6.37 \times 10^6 \) m. \( v_{\text{escape}} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 6.37 \times 10^6} \approx 11{,}180 \) m/s \( \approx \mathbf{11.2} \) km/s — about 33 times the speed of sound.

Energy conservation gives us \( v(x) \) — the speed at each position. Why do we still need numerical methods?
Explanation: Energy conservation is exact and gives us the escape velocity threshold. But it only relates \( v \) and \( x \), not \( x \) and \( t \). Questions like "how long does it take to reach the Moon?" or "what happens with air resistance?" require solving the full ODE \( \ddot{x} = -gR^2/x^2 \) in time — and that's where RK4 comes in (Section 4.16).

Python Code

Python 

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

g = 9.8         # m/s^2
R = 6.37e6      # m (Earth's radius)
v_esc = np.sqrt(2 * g * R)  # ~11,180 m/s

# v^2 as a function of x for different launch speeds
x = np.linspace(R, 20 * R, 1000)

fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2,
    subplot_titles=['v² vs Distance', 'Energy Diagram'])

for v0_kms in [8, 11.2, 15]:
    v0 = v0_kms * 1000
    v_sq = v0**2 - 2 * g * R * (1 - R / x)
    label = f'v₀ = {v0_kms} km/s'
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x/R, y=v_sq/1e6,
        name=label, mode='lines'), row=1, col=1)

fig.add_hline(y=0, line=dict(color='#808080', dash='dash'), row=1, col=1)

# Energy diagram
KE = lambda v0, x: 0.5 * v0**2 - g * R * (1 - R/x)
PE = -g * R**2 / x

for v0_kms in [8, 11.2, 15]:
    v0 = v0_kms * 1000
    E_total = 0.5 * v0**2 - g * R
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x/R, y=np.full_like(x, E_total/1e6),
        name=f'E (v₀={v0_kms})', line=dict(dash='dash'),
        showlegend=False), row=1, col=2)

fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x/R, y=PE/1e6,
    name='PE = −gR²/x', line=dict(color='#ff6b6b')), row=1, col=2)

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark')
fig.update_xaxes(title='x/R', gridcolor='#2a2f4a', linecolor='#808080')
fig.update_yaxes(title='(km/s)²', gridcolor='#2a2f4a', linecolor='#808080',
    row=1, col=1)
fig.update_yaxes(title='Energy/m (MJ/kg)', gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
    linecolor='#808080', row=1, col=2)
fig.show()

print(f"Escape velocity: {v_esc:.0f} m/s = {v_esc/1000:.1f} km/s")
Python 

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Solar system bodies: (name, g in m/s², R in meters)
bodies = {
    'Moon':    (1.62,  1.74e6),
    'Mars':    (3.72,  3.39e6),
    'Earth':   (9.80,  6.37e6),
    'Neptune': (11.15, 2.48e7),
    'Jupiter': (24.79, 7.15e7),
}

names, v_escs, colors = [], [], []
palette = ['#808080', '#ff6b6b', '#00f3ff', '#00ff9f', '#ff00ff']

for i, (name, (g_val, R_val)) in enumerate(bodies.items()):
    v_esc = np.sqrt(2 * g_val * R_val) / 1000  # km/s
    names.append(name)
    v_escs.append(v_esc)
    colors.append(palette[i])
    print(f"{name:10s}: g = {g_val:5.2f} m/s²,  R = {R_val:.2e} m,  "
          f"v_esc = {v_esc:.1f} km/s")

fig = go.Figure(go.Bar(
    x=names, y=v_escs,
    marker=dict(color=colors),
    text=[f'{v:.1f}' for v in v_escs],
    textposition='outside',
    textfont=dict(color='white', size=14)
))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    title='Escape Velocities Across the Solar System',
    yaxis=dict(title='Escape velocity (km/s)',
               gridcolor='#2a2f4a', linecolor='#808080'),
    xaxis=dict(linecolor='#808080')
)
fig.show()

What's Next?

Energy conservation tells us whether a projectile escapes (based on \( v_0 \) vs \( v_{\text{esc}} \)) and gives us \( v(x) \). But it doesn't answer: how long does the journey take? For that, we need to solve the full ODE \( \ddot{x} = -gR^2/x^2 \) as a function of time. In Section 4.15, we'll non-dimensionalize the problem, and in 4.16, we'll unleash RK4 to compute actual trajectories \( x(t) \).
