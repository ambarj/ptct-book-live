What Makes Things Oscillate?

A child on a swing, a guitar string, a current in an electrical circuit, atoms in a crystal — what do these have in common? They all oscillate: displaced from rest, they overshoot, reverse, overshoot again, and repeat. The remarkable fact is that every small oscillation around a stable equilibrium looks the same mathematically. In this section you'll discover why, and learn to read the three numbers that fully describe any simple harmonic motion.

Learning Objectives
  • Identify the three parameters of simple harmonic motion: amplitude \( A \), angular frequency \( \omega \), and phase \( \phi \)
  • Read \( A \), \( T \), and \( x(0) \) directly from an oscillation graph
  • Explain why a restoring force produces oscillation
  • Show that any potential energy minimum gives approximately harmonic motion for small displacements

Simple harmonic motion:

$$ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) $$

Three numbers tell the whole story: amplitude \( A \) (how far), angular frequency \( \omega \) (how fast, with period \( T = 2\pi/\omega \)), and phase \( \phi \) (where it starts).

The Three Parameters

Every simple harmonic oscillation is completely described by three numbers. Adjust them below and build intuition for what each one controls. Watch the green markers showing the period \( T \), the dashed yellow lines showing the amplitude \( A \), and the yellow dot showing the initial displacement \( x(0) = A\cos\phi \).

Interactive: Parameter Explorer
Key Insight

Amplitude \( A \) controls how far from equilibrium the object swings. Angular frequency \( \omega \) controls how fast it oscillates (with period \( T = 2\pi/\omega \) and frequency \( f = \omega/2\pi \)). Phase \( \phi \) controls where it starts at \( t = 0 \): when \( \phi = 0 \), the oscillation begins at its maximum; when \( \phi = 90° \), it begins crossing zero (going negative). Crucially, \( \omega \) depends on the system (spring stiffness, pendulum length), not on how hard you push — that's what makes it a natural frequency.

If you double the amplitude from A = 1 to A = 2, what happens to the period T?
Explanation
The period \( T = 2\pi/\omega \) depends only on \( \omega \), not on \( A \). Doubling the amplitude makes the oscillation swing wider, but it swings faster at the centre to compensate, keeping the period unchanged. This is a defining property of simple harmonic motion — the period is independent of amplitude. Try it!
You want the oscillator to complete one full cycle every 1.57 s (T = π/2). What ω do you need? Predict first, then set the slider and check with the green period markers.

ω = rad/s
Explanation
Invert \( T = 2\pi/\omega \): \( \omega = 2\pi/T = 2\pi/(\pi/2) = 4 \) rad/s. Set the slider to 4 and the green period markers land 1.57 s apart. Doubling ω halves T — frequency and period are reciprocal partners.
Find the phase φ that makes the motion start at the far negative turning point, x(0) = −A. (Set it, then confirm the yellow dot begins at the bottom of the swing.)
Explanation
\( x(0) = A\cos\phi = -A \) requires \( \cos\phi = -1 \), i.e. \( \phi = 180° \). (Check the neighbors: 90° starts at equilibrium moving backward, 270° at equilibrium moving forward, 0° at the positive turning point.) Phase is a starting-position dial: it slides the whole motion in time without changing its shape.

Why Things Oscillate: The Restoring Force

Oscillation requires a restoring force — a force that always pushes back toward equilibrium. Displace a spring to the right, and the spring pulls left. Displace a pendulum, and gravity pulls it back. The left panel shows the potential energy \( U(x) \), which has a minimum at equilibrium. The right panel shows the force \( F = -dU/dx \), which is negative for positive displacement and positive for negative displacement — always restoring.

Interactive: Restoring Force Lab
Key Insight

The dashed yellow curve shows the harmonic approximation: \( U(x) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}k_{\text{eff}}x^2 \). For the spring, this parabola is exact. For the pendulum and molecular bond, it’s only accurate near \( x = 0 \). Here’s the deep reason: if you zoom in close enough to the bottom of any smooth valley, it looks like a parabola. This is why all small oscillations are simple harmonic, regardless of the system. Try pushing the pendulum to large displacements (\( x > 2 \)) to see where the parabola stops matching.

Test Your Understanding

Use the Restoring Force Lab to answer these questions.

In the Spring system, drag the displacement slider to x = 1.5. What direction does the force point?
Explanation
For a spring, \( F = -kx \). When \( x > 0 \), the force is negative — it points back toward \( x = 0 \) (equilibrium). This is the defining property of a restoring force: it always acts opposite to the displacement. Without this, there would be no oscillation.
Switch to "Pendulum." At what displacement does the exact force (magenta) start to differ noticeably from the linear approximation (yellow dashed)?
Explanation
The exact pendulum force is \( -\sin(\theta) \) and the harmonic approximation replaces that with \( -\theta \). For small angles, sin(θ) and θ are nearly equal (try it on a calculator: sin(0.3) = 0.296). But by θ ≈ 1 radian (57°), sin(1) = 0.84 while the approximation says 1.0 — they differ by 16%. Rule of thumb: the approximation works well for swings smaller than about 30°.
Which statements about restoring forces are correct? (Select all that apply)
Explanation
  • True: Near the bottom of any smooth valley, the potential looks like a parabola: \( U(x) \approx U_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}k_{\text{eff}}(x-x_0)^2 \). The parabola fit works because the slope is zero at the minimum (that’s what makes it a minimum), so the first thing that matters is the curvature.
  • True: The curvature \( U''(x_0) \) plays the role of the spring constant. Steeper potentials give stiffer "springs" and faster oscillations.
  • False: It's only exact for a perfect spring (\( U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 \)). For all other systems, it's an approximation that improves as displacement shrinks.
  • True: The frequency \( \omega = \sqrt{k/m} \) depends on the spring constant and mass, not the amplitude. Push harder and the swing is wider, but not faster.

Read the Oscillation

A physicist's most basic skill: look at an oscillating signal and read off its parameters. Given a mystery oscillation, identify:

  • Amplitude A: the peak height (maximum displacement)
  • Period T: the time for one complete cycle (peak to next peak)
  • Initial displacement x(0): the value at \( t = 0 \)
Interactive: Pattern Matcher
Key Insight

To read \( A \): find the highest peak — that's the amplitude. To read \( T \): measure the time between two adjacent peaks (or two adjacent zero-crossings going the same direction). To read \( x(0) \): just look at where the curve starts at \( t = 0 \). If \( x(0) = A \), the phase is 0°; if \( x(0) = 0 \) (crossing downward), the phase is 90°; if \( x(0) = -A \), the phase is 180°.

A spring-mass system and a pendulum both oscillate with ω = 3 rad/s. Do they have the same motion?
Explanation
This is the universality of SHO: any system described by \( \ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0 \) has the solution \( x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) \), regardless of what physical mechanism creates the restoring force. A spring, a pendulum (for small angles), and an LC circuit all give exactly the same mathematical motion. The physics is different; the mathematics is identical.
Which physical systems exhibit simple harmonic motion (for small displacements)? (Select all that apply)
Explanation
  • True: The spring provides \( F = -kx \) — the textbook restoring force. \( \omega = \sqrt{k/m} \).
  • False: A hilltop is an unstable equilibrium (\( U''(x_0) < 0 \)). The force pushes away from equilibrium, causing runaway, not oscillation.
  • True: The LC circuit equation \( L\ddot{q} + q/C = 0 \) is an SHO with \( \omega = 1/\sqrt{LC} \). Charge oscillates between capacitor and inductor.
  • False: A uniform field has no equilibrium point — the particle accelerates uniformly. You need a potential minimum for oscillation.

Python Code

Here's the Python equivalent for exploring oscillation parameters and restoring forces.

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Parameters
A = 2.0       # Amplitude
omega = 2.0   # Angular frequency (rad/s)
phi = 0.0     # Phase (radians)
T = 2*np.pi / omega  # Period

t = np.linspace(0, 4*T, 1000)
x = A * np.cos(omega * t + phi)

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x, name='x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ)',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2.5)))

# Amplitude envelope
fig.add_hline(y=A, line=dict(color='#ffff00', dash='dash', width=1),
    annotation_text=f'A = {A}')
fig.add_hline(y=-A, line=dict(color='#ffff00', dash='dash', width=1))

# Period markers
fig.add_vline(x=0, line=dict(color='#00ff9f', dash='dot', width=1))
fig.add_vline(x=T, line=dict(color='#00ff9f', dash='dot', width=1),
    annotation_text=f'T = {T:.2f} s')

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title=f'SHM: A={A}, ω={omega} rad/s, φ={np.degrees(phi):.0f}°',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='x(t)', range=[-3.5, 3.5], gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
print(f"Period T = {T:.4f} s")
print(f"Frequency f = {omega/(2*np.pi):.4f} Hz")
print(f"x(0) = {A*np.cos(phi):.4f}")
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 500)

# Three systems
systems = {
    'Spring':    {'U': 0.5*x**2,       'F': -x,           'U_approx': 0.5*x**2},
    'Pendulum':  {'U': 1-np.cos(x),    'F': -np.sin(x),   'U_approx': 0.5*x**2},
    'Morse':     {'U': 2*(1-np.exp(-x))**2, 'F': -4*np.exp(-x)*(1-np.exp(-x)),
                  'U_approx': 2*x**2}
}

fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2,
    subplot_titles=['Potential Energy U(x)', 'Restoring Force F(x)'])

colors = {'Spring': '#00f3ff', 'Pendulum': '#ff00ff', 'Morse': '#00ff9f'}
for name, data in systems.items():
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=data['U'], name=f'{name}',
        line=dict(color=colors[name], width=2)), row=1, col=1)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=data['F'], name=f'{name} F',
        line=dict(color=colors[name], width=2), showlegend=False), row=1, col=2)

# Harmonic approximation (spring)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=0.5*x**2, name='½kx² (approx)',
    line=dict(color='#ffff00', width=1.5, dash='dash')), row=1, col=1)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=-x, name='-kx (approx)',
    line=dict(color='#ffff00', width=1.5, dash='dash'), showlegend=False), row=1, col=2)

fig.update_xaxes(title='x', gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
    zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080')
fig.update_yaxes(gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
    zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080')
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark', height=450)
fig.show()
