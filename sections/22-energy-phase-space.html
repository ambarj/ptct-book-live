3.3 Energy and Phase Space

💡
Learning Objectives:
  • Understand how kinetic and potential energy continuously exchange in SHM
  • Read phase portraits — the (x, v) plot that captures an oscillation's complete story
  • Connect orbit size in phase space to total energy and amplitude

So far we've described oscillations through position, velocity, and acceleration as functions of time. But there's a deeper perspective: energy. In simple harmonic motion, no energy is gained or lost — it simply flows back and forth between kinetic and potential forms. And there's a beautiful way to see this at a glance: the phase portrait.

The Energy Seesaw

For a unit-mass oscillator with \( x(t) = A\cos(\omega t) \):

$$ \text{PE} = \tfrac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2 \qquad \text{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2}v^2 \qquad E = \text{KE} + \text{PE} = \tfrac{1}{2}\omega^2 A^2 $$

The total energy \( E \) depends only on amplitude and frequency — it's constant throughout the motion.

Watch the left panel for position and the right panel for energy. When \( x \) is at a peak (maximum displacement), all energy is potential. When \( x \) crosses zero (maximum speed), all energy is kinetic. The yellow dashed line — total energy — never changes.

Explorer 1: Energy Tracker
Key Insight

Energy scales as \( E = \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 A^2 \). Doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy. Doubling the frequency also quadruples it. Try sliding each to see: at \( A = 3, \omega = 5 \), the energy reaches \( E = 112.5 \) — the yellow line shoots to the top of the panel.

Which statements about energy in SHM are true? (Select all that apply)
Explanation
  • Twice the frequency: True! Since \( \text{PE} \propto \cos^2(\omega t) = \frac{1}{2}(1 + \cos 2\omega t) \), the energy terms oscillate at \( 2\omega \). Count the PE peaks in one period of x(t) — there are two.
  • Always equal: False — they're only equal at specific instants. Most of the time one dominates.
  • Depends on phase: False — \( E = \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 A^2 \) has no \( \varphi \) dependence. Phase shifts when things happen, not the total energy.
  • PE = KE at \( A/\sqrt{2} \): True! When PE = KE = E/2, we need \( \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2 = \frac{1}{2}E = \frac{1}{4}\omega^2 A^2 \), giving \( x = \pm A/\sqrt{2} \).

Phase Space: The Complete Picture

So far we’ve been plotting position vs time. But there’s a more powerful way to look at an oscillator: plot position on one axis and velocity on the other. This is called phase space.

For simple harmonic motion, the phase-space trajectory is a closed loop — an ellipse. The left panel shows this ellipse (x vs v). The right panel rescales the velocity axis by dividing by ω, which turns the ellipse into a perfect circle of radius A.

$$ \frac{x^2}{A^2} + \frac{v^2}{(A\omega)^2} = 1 $$

This is the equation of an ellipse (just like \( x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 = 1 \) from algebra). If we rescale by dividing v by ω: \(\; x^2 + (v/\omega)^2 = A^2 \;\) — a circle whose radius equals the amplitude.

Explorer 2: Phase Space Explorer
Key Insight

The phase portrait tells you the entire history of the oscillation at a glance. Every point on the ellipse is a state (position + velocity). The orbit is traversed clockwise: from the rightmost point (max x, v = 0), the object speeds up leftward (v becomes negative), reaches maximum speed at x = 0, then decelerates, reaching the leftmost point (min x, v = 0), and returns.

⚠️
Why clockwise?

Think about what happens when the object is at the far right (x > 0, v = 0). The restoring force pulls it left, so v becomes negative — the trajectory moves downward. Following this logic around the loop, the orbit traces out clockwise. (Some textbooks plot momentum p = mv instead of v, which flips the convention — always check the axis labels!)

In the left panel (x, v), what happens to the ellipse shape when you increase \( \omega \) while keeping \( A \) fixed?
Explanation
The horizontal extent is \( \pm A \) (unchanged), but the vertical extent is \( \pm A\omega \). Increasing \( \omega \) stretches the ellipse vertically because the object reaches higher speeds. In the scaled plot (right), the circle stays the same size since the \( \omega \) factor is divided out.
In the right panel (scaled phase space), the orbit is always a circle. Its radius equals the: (read from the plot with A=2.0)
Explanation
The circle's radius equals the amplitude \( A = 2.0 \). Since \( x^2 + (v/\omega)^2 = A^2 \), the radius of the circle in scaled phase space is always exactly \( A \), regardless of \( \omega \). This is why the scaled representation is so useful — amplitude is directly visible as a geometric quantity.
A phase space orbit passes through the point \( (x=0, v=-6) \) with \( \omega = 2 \). What is the amplitude \( A \)?
Explanation
At \( x = 0 \), all energy is kinetic: \( |v| = A\omega \). So \( A = |v|/\omega = 6/2 = 3 \). You can verify: set A=3, \( \omega \)=2 in the explorer and check that the ellipse crosses the v-axis at \( \pm 6 \).

The energy bars grow quadratically: doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy. This is why the bar heights don't grow evenly — they follow the pattern 1, 4, 9, 16... (ratios of \( A^2 \)). Each orbit is a contour of constant energy, just like contour lines on a topographic map represent constant altitude.

Looking at the phase portrait, can two different orbits ever cross each other?
Explanation
Orbits never cross! Here’s why: a point in phase space specifies both position and velocity. If you know both of those, physics completely determines what happens next. So two different orbits can’t pass through the same point — that would mean two different futures from the same starting state, which contradicts the laws of motion.

What can you determine from a phase portrait alone, without knowing the time? (Select all that apply)
Explanation
  • Exact time: No — the phase portrait removes time information. All points on the orbit are visited, but you can't tell when.
  • Amplitude: Yes — it's the horizontal extent of the orbit (\( x_{\text{max}} = A \)).
  • Total energy: Yes — the orbit size encodes energy. Larger orbit = more energy.
  • Phase angle: No — \( \varphi \) only determines where on the orbit you start at \( t = 0 \). All starting points trace the same orbit.

Code It Yourself

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

A, omega = 2.0, 3.0
t = np.linspace(0, 4*np.pi/omega, 500)

x = A * np.cos(omega * t)
v = -A * omega * np.sin(omega * t)
KE = 0.5 * v**2
PE = 0.5 * omega**2 * x**2
E = 0.5 * omega**2 * A**2

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=PE, fill='tozeroy', name='PE',
    line=dict(color='#ff00ff'), fillcolor='rgba(255,0,255,0.15)'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=KE, fill='tozeroy', name='KE',
    line=dict(color='#00ff9f'), fillcolor='rgba(0,255,159,0.15)'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=np.full_like(t, E), name=f'E = {E:.1f}',
    line=dict(color='#ffff00', dash='dash', width=2)))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark', title='Energy in SHM',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Energy', range=[0, E*1.3],
               gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080',
               linecolor='#808080')
)
fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

omega = 2.0
theta = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 500)

fig = go.Figure()
colors = ['#00f3ff','#ff00ff','#ffff00','#00ff9f','#ff6b6b']

for i, A in enumerate([0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5]):
    x = A * np.cos(theta)
    v = -A * omega * np.sin(theta)
    E = 0.5 * omega**2 * A**2
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=v, mode='lines',
        name=f'A={A:.1f}, E={E:.1f}',
        line=dict(color=colors[i], width=2)))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    title='Phase Portrait: Nested Orbits',
    xaxis=dict(title='Position x', range=[-3, 3],
               gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080',
               linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Velocity v', range=[-6, 6],
               gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080',
               linecolor='#808080')
)
fig.show()
