Equipotentials and Field Lines

How does a topographic map show a 3D mountain range on a flat piece of paper? The answer is contour lines—curves connecting points of equal elevation. This same technique lets us visualize any scalar field: temperature distributions, air pressure, electric potential, and countless other quantities that have a single value at each point in space.

Learning Objectives
  • Understand how contour plots compress 3D information into 2D level curves
  • Read contour plots: identify maxima, minima, and gradients from line spacing
  • Visualize electric equipotentials and their relationship to field lines
  • Recognize that gradients are always perpendicular to contour lines
  • Connect contour visualization to physical applications

From 3D Surfaces to 2D Contours

A scalar field assigns a single number to each point in space: \( f(x, y) \). We can visualize this as a 3D surface where height represents the function value. But 3D plots can be hard to read precisely. Contour plots solve this by slicing the surface horizontally and projecting those slices onto a 2D plane.

Contour Line Definition:

$$ f(x, y) = c \quad \text{(constant)} $$

A contour line connects all points where the function equals the same value \( c \).

Reading Contour Plots
  • Closely spaced contours: Steep slope (rapid change)
  • Widely spaced contours: Gentle slope (gradual change)
  • Closed loops: Hills (maxima) or valleys (minima)
  • Saddle points: Where contours form an "X" pattern

Equipotentials: Contours in Physics

In electrostatics, the electric potential \( V(x, y) \) is a scalar field. Its contour lines are called equipotential lines—paths along which a charge can move without gaining or losing energy. The electric field \( \vec{E} \) is the negative gradient of potential: \( \vec{E} = -\nabla V \).

Electric Field from Potential:

$$ \vec{E} = -\nabla V = -\left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\hat{j} \right) $$

The electric field points in the direction of steepest decrease of potential.

Key Insight: Perpendicularity

Electric field lines are always perpendicular to equipotential lines. This isn't a coincidence—it's because the gradient (which gives the field direction) is mathematically perpendicular to level curves. A charge moving along an equipotential does no work; moving across equipotentials requires or releases energy.

What angle do electric field lines make with equipotential lines?
Explanation: Electric field lines are ALWAYS perpendicular (90°) to equipotential lines. This is because \( \vec{E} = -\nabla V \), and the gradient is always perpendicular to level curves of any scalar field. Toggle field lines on and observe this at any point!
In the dipole configuration, where is the potential exactly zero?
Explanation: On the perpendicular bisector, every point is equidistant from both charges. Since the charges are equal and opposite (+q and -q), their potentials (\( +k q/r \) and \( -k q/r \)) exactly cancel, giving V = 0. This shows up as the straight vertical equipotential line in the middle.
For the "Two Positive Charges" configuration, which statements are TRUE?
Explanation:
  • FALSE: Two positive charges both contribute positive potential—it never reaches zero.
  • TRUE: Far away, equipotentials merge into nearly circular shapes; closer, they form figure-8 patterns.
  • TRUE: The midpoint is a saddle point—potential decreases moving away perpendicular to the axis.
  • FALSE: Like charges repel! Field lines go outward from BOTH charges, never connecting them.
At the exact location of a point charge, the potential goes to .
Explanation: The potential of a point charge is \( V = kq/r \). As \( r \to 0 \), \( V \to \infty \) (for positive charges) or \( V \to -\infty \) (for negative). This is why we exclude the charge locations in the visualization—they're singularities!

The Gradient-Contour Connection

The gradient \( \nabla f \) of a scalar field points in the direction of steepest increase and has magnitude equal to the rate of change. Geometrically, the gradient is always perpendicular to contour lines. This is a fundamental relationship that appears throughout physics and mathematics.

Gradient in 2D:

$$ \nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\hat{j} $$

Points perpendicular to contours, toward increasing \( f \). Magnitude = slope steepness.

Common Misconception

The gradient does NOT point "along" the contour—it points across contours, perpendicular to them. Moving along a contour means zero change in \( f \), but the gradient points where \( f \) changes most rapidly. That's why they must be perpendicular!

Where contours are closely spaced, the gradient magnitude is .

Which statements about the gradient are TRUE? (Select all that apply)
Explanation:
  • TRUE: The gradient \( \nabla f \) is a vector with components \( (\partial f/\partial x, \partial f/\partial y) \).
  • TRUE: By definition, gradient points in the direction of steepest increase.
  • FALSE: Gradient is PERPENDICULAR to contours, not parallel!
  • TRUE: At critical points (maxima, minima, saddle points), \( \nabla f = 0 \).
Select the "Tilted Plane" function. What do you notice about the gradient field?
Explanation: For \( f(x,y) = 2x + y \), \( \nabla f = (2, 1) \) everywhere—a constant vector! The plane has uniform slope, so the gradient is the same at every point. The contours are equally-spaced parallel lines (perpendicular to the direction (2, 1)).

Python Code Examples

Create your own contour plots and explore scalar fields with these Python examples using Plotly.

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Create grid
x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
y = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)

# Scalar field: paraboloid
Z = X**2 + Y**2

# Create contour plot
fig = go.Figure(data=go.Contour(
    x=x, y=y, z=Z,
    colorscale='Viridis',
    contours=dict(
        showlabels=True,
        labelfont=dict(size=10, color='white')
    ),
    colorbar=dict(title='f(x,y)')
))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    title='Contour Plot: f(x,y) = x² + y²',
    xaxis=dict(title='x', scaleanchor='y'),
    yaxis=dict(title='y')
)

fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
import plotly.figure_factory as ff

# Create grid for contours
x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
y = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
Z = X**2 + Y**2

# Create grid for gradient arrows (coarser)
x_grad = np.linspace(-2.5, 2.5, 10)
y_grad = np.linspace(-2.5, 2.5, 10)
X_grad, Y_grad = np.meshgrid(x_grad, y_grad)

# Gradient components: ∇f = (2x, 2y)
U = 2 * X_grad
V = 2 * Y_grad

# Normalize for display
mag = np.sqrt(U**2 + V**2)
mag[mag == 0] = 1
U_norm = U / mag * 0.3
V_norm = V / mag * 0.3

# Create figure with contours
fig = go.Figure()

fig.add_trace(go.Contour(
    x=x, y=y, z=Z,
    colorscale='Viridis',
    showscale=False,
    contours=dict(showlabels=True)
))

# Add gradient arrows using annotations
for i in range(len(x_grad)):
    for j in range(len(y_grad)):
        fig.add_annotation(
            x=X_grad[j, i], y=Y_grad[j, i],
            ax=X_grad[j, i] + U_norm[j, i],
            ay=Y_grad[j, i] + V_norm[j, i],
            xref='x', yref='y', axref='x', ayref='y',
            showarrow=True, arrowhead=2, arrowsize=1.5,
            arrowcolor='#ff00ff'
        )

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    title='Contours with Gradient Vectors',
    xaxis=dict(range=[-3, 3], scaleanchor='y'),
    yaxis=dict(range=[-3, 3])
)

fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Create grid
x = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
y = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)

# Dipole potential: V = 1/r1 - 1/r2
d = 1.0  # charge separation
r1 = np.sqrt((X - d)**2 + Y**2)
r2 = np.sqrt((X + d)**2 + Y**2)

# Avoid singularities
r1[r1 < 0.1] = 0.1
r2[r2 < 0.1] = 0.1

V = 1/r1 - 1/r2

# Clip extreme values for visualization
V = np.clip(V, -5, 5)

fig = go.Figure(data=go.Contour(
    x=x, y=y, z=V,
    colorscale='RdBu_r',
    contours=dict(
        start=-4, end=4, size=0.5,
        showlabels=True
    ),
    colorbar=dict(title='V')
))

# Mark charge positions
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=[d, -d], y=[0, 0],
    mode='markers+text',
    marker=dict(size=15, color=['red', 'blue']),
    text=['+', '−'],
    textposition='top center',
    textfont=dict(size=20, color='white'),
    showlegend=False
))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    title='Electric Dipole Equipotentials',
    xaxis=dict(range=[-4, 4], scaleanchor='y'),
    yaxis=dict(range=[-4, 4])
)

fig.show()

Key Takeaways

Summary
  • Contour plots compress 3D scalar field information into 2D level curves
  • Closely spaced contours indicate steep slopes; widely spaced means flat regions
  • Equipotentials are contours of electric potential—charges move freely along them
  • Gradients are perpendicular to contour lines and point toward increasing values
  • Electric fields are perpendicular to equipotentials: \( \vec{E} = -\nabla V \)
