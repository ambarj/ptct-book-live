One Equation, Many Systems

A mass on a spring, a swinging pendulum, a current sloshing back and forth in an electrical circuit — these look nothing alike physically. Yet if you write down the equation of motion for each, the same equation appears every time:

$$ \ddot{x} = -\omega^2 x $$

The variable \( x \) changes its name — displacement, angle, charge — but the mathematics is identical. Once you've solved it for one system, you've solved it for all of them.

Learning Objectives
  • Map the SHO equation onto three physical systems: spring-mass, pendulum, and LC circuit
  • Convert parameters between domains (k, m ↔ g, L ↔ L, C)
  • Recognize that the derivative always leads the primary variable by \( \pi/2 \)
  • Appreciate that solving one system gives the solution for all

Three Systems, One Pattern

Let's see universality in action. Below, three completely different physical systems oscillate side by side. Adjust their physical parameters and watch how the natural frequency \( \omega \) changes. The remarkable fact: the oscillation shape is always a cosine — only the frequency changes.

Spring: \( \omega = \sqrt{k/m} \)   Pendulum: \( \omega = \sqrt{g/L} \)   LC circuit: \( \omega = 1/\sqrt{LC} \)

Explorer 1: System Identifier
Key Insight

A stiff spring (large \( k \)) oscillates fast, just like a short pendulum (small \( L \)) or a small LC circuit (small \( L_c C \)). The physics is different, but the math is the same: larger restoring tendency means faster oscillation.

Test Your Understanding

Use the System Identifier to answer these questions.

You double the spring constant \( k \) while keeping mass \( m \) fixed. What happens to the natural frequency \( \omega \)?
Explanation
Since \( \omega = \sqrt{k/m} \), doubling \( k \) multiplies \( \omega \) by \( \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414 \). The square root means you need to quadruple \( k \) to double \( \omega \). Try it: set k from 4 to 8 and watch the cyan curve speed up.
A pendulum has length L = 1 m, so \( \omega = \sqrt{9.8} \approx 3.13 \) rad/s. What length gives \( \omega = 2 \times 3.13 = 6.26 \) rad/s?

L = m
Explanation
Since \( \omega = \sqrt{g/L} \), doubling \( \omega \) requires \( L \to L/4 \). So \( L = 1/4 = 0.25 \) m. A shorter pendulum swings faster — just like a stiffer spring. The analogy is "shorter pendulum = stiffer spring".
In an LC circuit, you replace the capacitor with one of four times the capacitance. How does the oscillation period change?
Explanation
\( \omega = 1/\sqrt{LC} \), so \( T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC} \). Replacing \( C \to 4C \) gives \( T \to 2\pi\sqrt{4LC} = 2T \). The period doubles. Larger capacitance = more charge to move = slower oscillation, analogous to a heavier mass on a spring.

Translating Between Worlds

The universality of \( \ddot{x} = -\omega^2 x \) means we can "translate" between systems. Given a spring with certain \( k \) and \( m \), what pendulum length gives the same oscillation frequency? What LC circuit? The answer is always the same: match \( \omega \).

Same \( \omega \) means:   \( \displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L_c C}} \)

Explorer 2: Cross-Domain Mapper
Key Insight

The "Rosetta stone" for oscillations is the natural frequency \( \omega \). Every system has its own formula for \( \omega \) in terms of physical parameters, but once you know \( \omega \), the motion is \( x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \) regardless of what's oscillating.

Test Your Understanding

Use the Cross-Domain Mapper to answer these questions.

You need the spring-mass system to tick at \( \omega = 4.0 \) rad/s with m = 1 kg. What k do you need?

k = N/m
Explanation
Invert \( \omega = \sqrt{k/m} \): you need \( k = m\omega^2 = 1 \times 4^2 = 16 \) N/m. Set k = 16, m = 1 in the mapper and watch \( \omega \) land on 4.0. Notice the square: to double a spring's frequency you must make it four times stiffer — frequency is expensive in stiffness.
A grandfather-clock pendulum must complete one full swing in exactly T = 2.0 s. What length L do you need? Hint: \( T = 2\pi/\omega \) and \( \omega = \sqrt{g/L} \).

L = m
Explanation
You need \( \omega = 2\pi/T = \pi \) rad/s, and inverting \( \omega = \sqrt{g/L} \) gives \( L = g/\omega^2 = 9.8/\pi^2 \approx 0.99 \) m. Set L just under 1 m in the mapper and check that T reads 2.0 s. It's a lovely coincidence that \( g \approx \pi^2 \) in SI units — it's why grandfather clocks are about a meter tall.
In the spring analogy for an LC circuit, what plays the role of the spring constant \( k \)?
Explanation
The LC circuit equation is \( L\ddot{Q} + Q/C = 0 \). Compare it side by side with the spring: \( m\ddot{x} + kx = 0 \). They have the same structure! Inductance L plays the role of mass m (it resists changes in current, just like mass resists changes in velocity). And 1/C plays the role of spring constant k (a small capacitor “pushes back” harder, like a stiff spring).

The Universal Phase Lag

In every SHO system, the derivative of the oscillating quantity lags behind (or leads) by exactly \( \pi/2 \) — one quarter of a period. For a spring: velocity leads position by 90°. For an LC circuit: current \( I = dQ/dt \) leads charge \( Q \) by 90°. Switch between systems below and watch: the phase relationship is always the same.

Explorer 3: Phase Relationship Explorer
Key Insight

The 90° phase lag is not a coincidence — it’s built into how cosines work. Velocity is the derivative of position. If position is a cosine, velocity is a sine (from calculus: d/dt[cos(ωt)] = −ωsin(ωt)). And sine is just cosine shifted by a quarter period — that’s exactly 90°. This relationship holds for every SHO system, whether it’s a spring, a pendulum, or a circuit.

Explanation
When Q is maximum, all energy is stored in the capacitor (like PE at maximum displacement). The current, which is \( I = dQ/dt \), is zero at that moment — just like velocity is zero when a spring is at maximum extension. Switch to "Spring" mode to see the exact same pattern with x and v.

The derivative peaks \( \Delta t = \pi/(2\omega) \) after the primary variable. Using the explorer, find the \( \omega \) for which this delay is exactly 0.5 s. Verify with the yellow dashed markers.

ω = rad/s
Explanation
Invert \( \Delta t = \pi/(2\omega) \): \( \omega = \pi/(2 \times 0.5) = \pi \approx 3.14 \) rad/s. At that setting the yellow dashed markers sit exactly 0.5 s apart. The delay is always one quarter of the period (\( T/4 = \pi/(2\omega) \)), so asking for a 0.5 s lag is the same as asking for a 2-second period — the derivative always runs a quarter-cycle ahead, whatever the units.

Code It Yourself

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

# Physical parameters
k, m = 4.0, 1.0        # spring
L_pend = 1.0            # pendulum length
L_c, C = 0.1, 0.01     # LC circuit

# Natural frequencies
omega_spring = np.sqrt(k / m)
omega_pend = np.sqrt(9.8 / L_pend)
omega_lc = 1.0 / np.sqrt(L_c * C)

t = np.linspace(0, 15, 500)

fig = go.Figure()
for omega, name, color in [
    (omega_spring, f'Spring (ω={omega_spring:.2f})', '#00f3ff'),
    (omega_pend, f'Pendulum (ω={omega_pend:.2f})', '#ff00ff'),
    (omega_lc, f'LC (ω={omega_lc:.2f})', '#ffff00')]:
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=np.cos(omega*t),
        name=name, line=dict(color=color, width=2)))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title='Three Systems, One Equation',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Normalized displacement', range=[-1.5, 1.5]))
fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np

def match_frequency(omega_target, system='pendulum'):
    """Find parameters to match a target frequency."""
    if system == 'pendulum':
        L = 9.8 / omega_target**2
        print(f"Pendulum: L = {L:.4f} m")
    elif system == 'spring':
        # Choose m=1 kg
        k = omega_target**2
        print(f"Spring: k = {k:.4f} N/m (m = 1 kg)")
    elif system == 'lc':
        # Choose C = 1 mF
        C = 1e-3
        Lc = 1.0 / (omega_target**2 * C)
        print(f"LC: L = {Lc:.4f} H, C = {C*1e3:.1f} mF")

# Example: spring with k=10, m=2 → match other systems
omega = np.sqrt(10 / 2)  # = √5 ≈ 2.236
print(f"Source: spring k=10, m=2 → ω = {omega:.3f}")
match_frequency(omega, 'pendulum')
match_frequency(omega, 'lc')
