Applications: Diffusion in the Real World

One equation, many worlds. The diffusion equation \( \partial u / \partial t = D\,\nabla^2 u \) appears wherever microscopic randomness produces macroscopic spreading. Change the name of \( u \), plug in a different \( D \), and you describe heat conduction, molecular diffusion, pollutant dispersal, doping profiles in semiconductors, or even stock-price fluctuations. This section tours the applications and builds a single computational tool that handles them all.

Learning Objectives
  • Recognize the diffusion equation in at least five different scientific domains
  • Use the scaling relation \( \ell \sim \sqrt{Dt} \) to make quick estimates in any field
  • Understand that same equation = same solution — only the physical interpretation of \( u \) and \( D \) changes
  • Appreciate the power of mathematical universality: one derivation serves all applications

The Universal Equation:

$$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^2 u $$

Heat: \( u = T \), \( D = \kappa \) (thermal diffusivity).   Mass: \( u = c \), \( D = D_\text{mol} \).   Finance: \( u = P \), \( D = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \).

To double the diffusion distance, you must wait…
Explanation: \( \ell = \sqrt{2Dt} \), so \( t = \ell^2/(2D) \). Doubling \( \ell \) quadruples \( t \). This is why diffusion is slow over long distances — it is efficient only over short ones. Nature uses convection (blood flow, wind) to beat the diffusion speed limit.
O₂ in air (\( D = 2 \times 10^{-5} \) m²/s). How far does it diffuse in 1 minute? Set the sliders and read: \( \ell \approx \) cm.
Explanation: \( \ell = \sqrt{2 \times 2 \times 10^{-5} \times 60} = \sqrt{2.4 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 0.049 \) m = 4.9 cm. Even gas-phase diffusion only reaches a few centimeters in a minute — that is why you can smell dinner from the kitchen but not from across the street (convection carries most of it).
A semiconductor fab heats silicon to increase \( D \) from \( 10^{-16} \) to \( 10^{-12} \) m²/s. The doping time for the same depth is reduced by…
Explanation: At fixed depth \( \ell \), \( t = \ell^2/(2D) \propto 1/D \). Increasing \( D \) by \( 10^4 \) reduces \( t \) by the same factor. This is why semiconductor doping furnaces run at 1000°C+ — it is the only way to achieve practical processing times.

Which are true about mathematical universality? (Select all)
Explanation:
  • One solution serves all: ✓ The Gaussian fundamental solution, Fourier methods, numerical schemes — all transfer instantly between domains.
  • Different methods: ✗ The same PDE has the same solutions. Boundary conditions may differ, but the mathematical toolkit is shared.
  • Cross-disciplinary reading: ✓ This is the power of mathematical physics — shared language across fields.
  • Same D: ✗ Universality means same equation, not same parameters. \( D \) varies by 15 orders of magnitude across our examples!
Chapter 7 Summary: From Steps to PDEs

We began with a single drunk step (±1) and built an entire theoretical framework: random walk → binomial distribution → (Stirling) → Gaussian → diffusion equation. Along the way we learned that microscopic randomness produces macroscopic order — the Gaussian and \( \sqrt{t} \) scaling are universal. These ideas underpin statistical mechanics, financial mathematics, biological transport, and engineering design. The diffusion equation is where random walks meet partial differential equations — and computational thinking ties it all together.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

systems = [
    ('Heat in iron',     2.3e-5, 's', 0.1),
    ('Ink in water',     1e-9,   's', 0.01),
    ('O₂ in air',        2e-5,   's', 0.1),
    ('Dopant in Si',     1e-16,  's', 1e-7),
]
colors = ['#00f3ff', '#ff006e', '#ffbe0b', '#00ff88']

fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2,
    subplot_titles=['Gaussian profiles (t=1 unit)', 'RMS distance vs time'])

for i, (name, D, unit, xmax) in enumerate(systems):
    x = np.linspace(-xmax, xmax, 300)
    rho = np.exp(-x**2/(4*D*1)) / np.sqrt(4*np.pi*D*1)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=rho, mode='lines',
        line=dict(color=colors[i], width=2), name=name), row=1, col=1)

    t = np.linspace(0.01, 10, 100)
    rms = np.sqrt(2*D*t)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=rms, mode='lines',
        line=dict(color=colors[i], width=2), name=name,
        showlegend=False), row=1, col=2)

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark', height=400)
for ax in ['xaxis', 'xaxis2', 'yaxis', 'yaxis2']:
    fig.update_layout(**{ax: dict(zerolinecolor='#808080')})
fig.show()
Python

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Scaling: ℓ = √(2Dt)  →  t = ℓ²/(2D)  →  D = ℓ²/(2t)
def diffusion_distance(D, t):
    return np.sqrt(2 * D * t)

def diffusion_time(D, ell):
    return ell**2 / (2 * D)

# Example: how long for ink to diffuse 1 cm?
D_ink = 1e-9  # m²/s
ell = 0.01    # 1 cm in meters
t = diffusion_time(D_ink, ell)
print(f"Ink diffuses 1 cm in {t:.0f} s = {t/3600:.1f} hours")

# Sweep: distance vs time for multiple systems
systems = {'Heat/iron': 2.3e-5, 'O₂/air': 2e-5,
           'Ink/water': 1e-9, 'Dopant/Si': 1e-16}
t = np.logspace(-2, 8, 200)

fig = go.Figure()
for name, D in systems.items():
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=diffusion_distance(D, t),
        mode='lines', line=dict(width=2), name=f'{name} (D={D:.0e})'))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='time (s)', type='log', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='distance (m)', type='log', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    height=400)
fig.show()
