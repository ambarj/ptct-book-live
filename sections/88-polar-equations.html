Polar Coordinates & The Radial Equation

Cartesian coordinates were a great choice in Section 8.1 — they made the two-body reduction transparent. But for central forces, where the force vector points along \( \hat r \) and the potential depends only on \( |\vec r| \), Cartesian \( (x, y) \) hides the rotational symmetry. Polar coordinates \( (\rho, \phi) \) reveal it. We will derive the equations of motion in polar form, watch angular-momentum conservation pop out as the φ-equation without ever invoking torque, and use it to eliminate \( \dot\phi \) in favor of the constant \( \ell \). What's left is a single second-order ODE in \( \rho(t) \) — and it carries an extra term that looks like a force pushing outward: the famous centrifugal barrier.

💡
Learning Objectives:
  • Decompose velocity and acceleration into radial and tangential components in polar coordinates
  • Identify the four polar-acceleration terms and know which vanish when (circular orbit, radial fall, etc.)
  • Derive the polar EOM \( \mu(\ddot\rho - \rho\dot\phi^2) = f(\rho) \) and \( \mu(\rho\ddot\phi + 2\dot\rho\dot\phi) = 0 \)
  • Recognize that the φ-equation is angular-momentum conservation: \( \ell = \mu\rho^2\dot\phi = \text{const} \)
  • Use ℓ to eliminate \( \dot\phi \) and obtain the radial equation \( \mu\ddot\rho = -dU/d\rho + \ell^2/(\mu\rho^3) \) with its centrifugal term

Velocity and Acceleration in Polar Coordinates

The polar basis vectors \( \hat\rho \) and \( \hat\phi \) are orthonormal, but unlike \( \hat x \) and \( \hat y \), they rotate with the particle: \( \hat\rho \) always points outward from the origin to the particle, and \( \hat\phi \) points in the direction of increasing angle. Because the basis vectors themselves change in time, when we differentiate position to get velocity (and again to get acceleration), we pick up extra terms from \( d\hat\rho/dt = \dot\phi\,\hat\phi \) and \( d\hat\phi/dt = -\dot\phi\,\hat\rho \).

Position:

$$ \vec r = \rho\,\hat\rho $$

Velocity (one differentiation):

$$ \vec v = \dot\rho\,\hat\rho + \rho\dot\phi\,\hat\phi, \qquad v^2 = \dot\rho^2 + \rho^2\dot\phi^2 $$

Acceleration (two differentiations):

$$ \vec a = \underbrace{(\ddot\rho - \rho\dot\phi^2)}_{\text{radial}}\,\hat\rho + \underbrace{(\rho\ddot\phi + 2\dot\rho\dot\phi)}_{\text{tangential}}\,\hat\phi $$
💡
Where Do the Weird Terms Come From?

The acceleration has four terms, not just \( \ddot\rho \) and \( \rho\ddot\phi \). The two extra terms — \( -\rho\dot\phi^2 \) (centripetal) and \( 2\dot\rho\dot\phi \) (Coriolis) — come from the rotation of the basis vectors themselves. They are not extra physical forces; they are bookkeeping costs of using a rotating coordinate system.

Sanity check: on a uniform circular orbit, \( \dot\rho = 0 \) and \( \rho \) is constant, so the only surviving term is \( -\rho\dot\phi^2\,\hat\rho \) — the familiar centripetal acceleration \( v^2/\rho \) pointing inward. ✓

The Polar Equations of Motion

Newton's second law for the equivalent one-body problem is \( \mu\ddot{\vec r} = \vec f(r) \). For a central force, \( \vec f = f(\rho)\,\hat\rho \) — purely radial, no \( \hat\phi \) component. Writing both sides in the polar basis:

Polar equations of motion:

$$ \mu(\ddot\rho - \rho\dot\phi^2) = f(\rho) \qquad \text{(radial)} $$ $$ \mu(\rho\ddot\phi + 2\dot\rho\dot\phi) = 0 \qquad \text{(tangential)} $$

Two coupled second-order ODEs in \( \rho(t) \) and \( \phi(t) \). The radial equation has the central force on the right-hand side; the tangential equation has zero on the right because the central force has no \( \hat\phi \) component.

The φ-equation looks complicated, but it is hiding a clean conservation law. Multiply both sides by \( \rho \):

$$ \rho \cdot \mu(\rho\ddot\phi + 2\dot\rho\dot\phi) = \mu(\rho^2\ddot\phi + 2\rho\dot\rho\dot\phi) = \mu \cdot \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\rho^2\dot\phi\right) = 0 $$

The expression in parentheses on the second equality is exactly \( d(\rho^2\dot\phi)/dt \) by the product rule (try it!). So

$$ \boxed{\ell \equiv \mu\rho^2\dot\phi = \text{const}} $$
💡
Key Insight: the φ-EOM is angular-momentum conservation.

We just derived \( \ell = \mu\rho^2\dot\phi = \text{const} \) without invoking torque, cross products, or the angular-momentum theorem. It fell out of the tangential equation of motion because central forces have no φ-component. This is the same ℓ from Section 8.2 — but here it appears as an algebraic consequence of choosing the right coordinates rather than a symmetry argument. Both viewpoints are correct; both are useful.

Eliminating \( \dot\phi \): The Radial Equation

Conservation of \( \ell \) means \( \dot\phi = \ell/(\mu\rho^2) \). We can substitute this into the radial equation to eliminate \( \dot\phi \) entirely, leaving a single ODE in \( \rho(t) \):

$$ \mu(\ddot\rho - \rho\dot\phi^2) = f(\rho) \;\Rightarrow\; \mu\ddot\rho = f(\rho) + \mu\rho\dot\phi^2 = f(\rho) + \mu\rho \cdot \frac{\ell^2}{\mu^2\rho^4} $$

For a conservative central force, \( f(\rho) = -dU/d\rho \), so:

$$ \boxed{\mu\ddot\rho = -\frac{dU}{d\rho} + \frac{\ell^2}{\mu\rho^3}} $$

The extra term \( +\ell^2/(\mu\rho^3) \) is the centrifugal force. It pushes outward (positive sign) and grows like \( 1/\rho^3 \) as the particle approaches the origin. For any \( \ell > 0 \), the centrifugal term diverges at \( \rho \to 0 \) — creating an "angular-momentum barrier" that prevents the orbiting particle from ever reaching the origin (no matter how attractive \( U(\rho) \) is, as long as it's not more singular than \( 1/\rho^2 \)).

Explanation: Set \( -k/\rho^2 + \ell^2/(\mu\rho^3) = 0 \) ⇒ \( \ell^2/(\mu\rho^3) = k/\rho^2 \) ⇒ \( \rho_{\text{circ}} = \ell^2/(\mu k) \). Notice how increasing \( \ell \) increases the orbital radius (more angular momentum → bigger orbit), and increasing \( k \) (stronger gravity) decreases it (gravity wins, orbit shrinks).
Why is the centrifugal term \( +\ell^2/(\mu\rho^3) \) called fictitious? (Select all that apply.)
Explanation:
  • Not in Cartesian: ✓ In Cartesian coordinates, the EOM is just \( m\ddot{\vec r} = \vec F \) — no centrifugal term anywhere.
  • Real physical force: ✗ It is not a fundamental force. It's a bookkeeping term that appears because polar coordinates aren't inertial in the same way Cartesian are.
  • Comes from rotation: ✓ Closely related to centrifugal forces in rotating reference frames in introductory mechanics.
  • Consequences are real: ✓ Don't dismiss it — it determines real physical observables like circular-orbit radii and the size of the Kepler problem's "well."

What's Next: The Effective Potential

💡
Forward Reference: a 1-D problem in disguise.

The radial equation \( \mu\ddot\rho = -dU/d\rho + \ell^2/(\mu\rho^3) \) looks like 1-D Newtonian motion in some new potential. We just have to identify it. Notice that \( -d/d\rho\,[U(\rho) + \ell^2/(2\mu\rho^2)] = -dU/d\rho + \ell^2/(\mu\rho^3) \) — the right-hand side is exactly the negative gradient of a single function. That function is the effective potential:

$$ U_{\text{eff}}(\rho) = U(\rho) + \frac{\ell^2}{2\mu\rho^2} $$

and the radial equation becomes \( \mu\ddot\rho = -dU_{\text{eff}}/d\rho \) — a particle of mass μ moving in 1-D in the potential \( U_{\text{eff}} \). Section 8.4 will plot this potential for various choices of \( U(\rho) \) and use it to read off circular-orbit radii, turning points, and bound vs unbound orbits — by inspection, without ever solving an ODE.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Convert Cartesian (x,y,vx,vy) to polar (ρ, φ, ρ̇, φ̇), and back.
def cart_to_polar(x, y, vx, vy):
    rho   = np.sqrt(x**2 + y**2)
    phi   = np.arctan2(y, x)
    # ρ̇ = (x vx + y vy) / ρ; φ̇ = (x vy − y vx) / ρ²
    rdot  = (x*vx + y*vy) / rho
    pdot  = (x*vy - y*vx) / rho**2
    return rho, phi, rdot, pdot

def polar_to_cart(rho, phi, rdot, pdot):
    x = rho * np.cos(phi);  y = rho * np.sin(phi)
    # v = ρ̇ ρ̂ + ρ φ̇ φ̂; ρ̂ = (cos φ, sin φ), φ̂ = (−sin φ, cos φ)
    vx = rdot * np.cos(phi) - rho * pdot * np.sin(phi)
    vy = rdot * np.sin(phi) + rho * pdot * np.cos(phi)
    return x, y, vx, vy

# Sanity check: round-trip
x0, y0, vx0, vy0 = 1.0, 0.5, -0.2, 0.7
rho, phi, rd, pd = cart_to_polar(x0, y0, vx0, vy0)
xb, yb, vxb, vyb = polar_to_cart(rho, phi, rd, pd)
print(f"original: ({x0:.3f}, {y0:.3f}, {vx0:.3f}, {vy0:.3f})")
print(f"recovered: ({xb:.3f}, {yb:.3f}, {vxb:.3f}, {vyb:.3f})")
print(f"|v|² = {vx0**2 + vy0**2:.4f}  (Cartesian)")
print(f"ρ̇² + ρ²φ̇² = {rd**2 + (rho*pd)**2:.4f}  (polar)")

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Integrate Kepler in Cartesian, then verify ρ²φ̇ is constant in time.
mu = 1.0
def F(r):  return -r / (r @ r) ** 1.5

def rhs(s):
    return np.concatenate([s[2:], F(s[:2])])

def rk4_step(s, h):
    k1 = rhs(s); k2 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k1)
    k3 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k2); k4 = rhs(s + h*k3)
    return s + (h/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

s = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.9])
h, N = 0.02, 1500
traj = np.zeros((N, 4))
for k in range(N):
    traj[k] = s; s = rk4_step(s, h)

x, y, vx, vy = traj[:, 0], traj[:, 1], traj[:, 2], traj[:, 3]
rho   = np.sqrt(x**2 + y**2)
pdot  = (x*vy - y*vx) / rho**2
ell   = mu * rho**2 * pdot

t = np.arange(N) * h
fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=ell, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2), name='ℓ = μρ²φ̇ (measured)'))
fig.add_hline(y=ell[0], line=dict(color='#ff006e', dash='dash'),
              annotation_text=f'ℓ₀ = {ell[0]:.4f}')
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='t', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='ℓ', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    title='Angular momentum is constant — the φ-EOM in action',
    height=400)
fig.show()
print(f"ℓ relative drift: {np.max(np.abs((ell - ell[0])/ell[0])):.2e}")

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Plot U(ρ), centrifugal term ℓ²/(2μρ²), and their sum for various ℓ.
mu = 1.0
k  = 1.0
rho = np.linspace(0.1, 8, 500)
U_kepler = -k / rho

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=rho, y=U_kepler, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2), name='U(ρ) = −k/ρ'))
for ell, color in zip([0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5], ['#888', '#00ff88', '#ffbe0b', '#ff006e']):
    Ueff = U_kepler + ell**2 / (2 * mu * rho**2)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=rho, y=Ueff, mode='lines',
        line=dict(color=color, width=2),
        name=f'U + ℓ²/(2μρ²), ℓ = {ell}'))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='ρ', range=[0, 8], zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='U_eff', range=[-1.5, 1.5], zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    title='Centrifugal barrier raises the effective potential at small ρ',
