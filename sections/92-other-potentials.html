Other Central Potentials

Bertrand's theorem (1873) told us that only two central potentials produce closed orbits for all bounded initial conditions: the Kepler potential \( U \propto -1/\rho \) and the 3-D harmonic oscillator \( U \propto \rho^2 \). Every other attractive central force gives precessing rosettes — orbits that look almost-elliptical but never quite close. This section is the experimental confirmation. We'll plug Coulomb-repulsion, the harmonic spring, the Yukawa (screened-Coulomb) potential, and the Lennard-Jones molecular potential into the U_eff machinery (§ 8.4) and the live RK4 integrator (§ 8.6) — no code change needed beyond a one-line force swap. The orbits speak for themselves.

A Catalog of Central Potentials

Four canonical non-Kepler systems that occur all over physics:

Coulomb-repulsive (like-charges):

$$ U(\rho) = +\frac{k_C\,q_1 q_2}{\rho} \qquad (k_C\,q_1q_2 > 0) $$

3-D Harmonic Oscillator / Spring:

$$ U(\rho) = \tfrac{1}{2}K\rho^2 $$

Yukawa (screened Coulomb) — nuclear strong force, plasmas:

$$ U(\rho) = -\frac{k}{\rho}\,e^{-\rho/\rho_0} $$

Lennard-Jones — molecular interactions:

$$ U(\rho) = 4\varepsilon\!\left[\!\left(\frac{\sigma}{\rho}\right)^{\!12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{\rho}\right)^{\!6}\right] $$

Each appears in a different corner of physics. Coulomb-repulsion governs Rutherford scattering (alpha particles bouncing off gold nuclei). The harmonic spring is two masses tethered by an idealized spring — and the small-oscillation limit of any potential well. Yukawa is the workhorse of nuclear physics: it's the form Hideki Yukawa proposed in 1935 for the strong force between nucleons (predicting the pion's mass from \( \rho_0 \)). Lennard-Jones models intermolecular interactions in liquids and gases — its minimum sets the equilibrium bond length, and the second derivative there gives the molecular vibration frequency.

💡
Bertrand's Theorem Recap.

Of all attractive central potentials, only \( U \propto -1/\rho \) (Kepler) and \( U \propto \rho^2 \) (harmonic) produce closed orbits for all bounded initial conditions. Three immediate consequences:

  • Mercury's perihelion precesses by ~43"/century beyond Newtonian predictions — a small \( -1/\rho^3 \) correction in Einstein's general relativity explains it exactly.
  • Newton's gravity is special. The exact \( 1/r^2 \) force is one of only two integrable cases. Any deviation from inverse-square instantly produces precession.
  • Molecular vibrations look pendulum-like. Near the bottom of a Lennard-Jones (or any) well, the curvature is locally quadratic — small oscillations are simple harmonic. This is why so much of physics looks like SHO.

Test Your Understanding

Use the Bertrand Demonstrator.

The harmonic-spring orbit \( U = \tfrac{1}{2}K\rho^2 \) (force toward the origin proportional to displacement) gives a closed orbit. What's the geometric difference from a Kepler ellipse?
Explanation: For the 3-D harmonic oscillator, the orbit is an ellipse centered on the origin (its two foci sit symmetrically at ±ae from the force center). In polar form ρ(φ) = b/√(1−e²cos²φ) — minimum and maximum radius each occur twice per revolution, so the radial oscillation repeats every Δφ = π (two perihelia per revolution) and the full orbit closes after 2π. Kepler ρ(φ) = p/(1+e cos φ) has minimum and maximum once per orbit, force center at one focus, closing only after \( 2\pi \). Both close — but in geometrically distinct ways. Both are blessed by Bertrand's theorem.
Mercury's perihelion advances by ~43"/century beyond Newton's prediction (after subtracting effects from other planets). The leading explanation is …
Explanation: Le Verrier (in 1859) postulated planet "Vulcan" to explain Mercury's anomalous precession; it was never found. Einstein (in 1915) showed general relativity adds a small \( -1/\rho^3 \) correction to the effective Newtonian potential — a non-Bertrand term that makes orbits precess. The predicted rate matched the observed 43"/century to within measurement precision. This was one of GR's first triumphs and a vindication of Bertrand's theorem: any deviation from \( 1/\rho \) — even relativistic corrections — produces precession.
Which statements about Bertrand's theorem and physical systems are true? (Select all.)
Explanation:
  • Yukawa precesses: ✓ It's not a power law and contains an exponential; not Bertrand-blessed.
  • LJ ≈ harmonic near minimum: ✓ Taylor-expand any potential around its minimum and the leading term is quadratic. This is why the SHO is omnipresent in physics.
  • Bertrand statement: ✓ Exactly the theorem.
  • Precessing = chaotic: ✗ Precession is regular and quasi-periodic — predictable. Chaos is sensitive dependence on initial conditions and exponential divergence — irregular. Mercury's orbit precesses but is not chaotic.
  • QM requires Bertrand: ✗ QM bound states exist for any well — Yukawa, LJ, square-well, etc. Bertrand is about classical orbital closure, a different question entirely.
💡
Key Insight: One Integrator, Many Worlds.

We just changed one function — the central force — and the same RK4 integrator handled four wildly different physical systems: like-charge electrostatic flyby, vibrating diatomic molecule, screened nuclear interaction, near-Kepler precession. This is the universality of the numerical approach. Section 8.5's analytical conic was a beautiful and rare exception. Most central potentials in real physics — molecular Lennard-Jones, nuclear Yukawa, relativistic Schwarzschild — admit no closed-form orbital solution. The numerical integrator is the only path forward, and you now have it.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Generic RK4 central-force integrator. Force passed as a callable F(r) → -∇U.
def make_F(potential, **p):
    if potential == 'kepler':         # U = -k/r,  F = -k r̂/r²
        return lambda r: -p['k'] * r / (r @ r) ** 1.5
    if potential == 'harmonic':       # U = ½K r², F = -K r
        return lambda r: -p['K'] * r
    if potential == 'yukawa':         # U = -k e^(-r/r0)/r
        def F_y(r):
            rmag = np.sqrt(r @ r)
            return -p['k'] * r * np.exp(-rmag/p['r0']) * (
                1/rmag**3 + 1/(p['r0']*rmag**2))
        return F_y
    if potential == 'power101':       # U = -k/r^(1.01)
        return lambda r: -1.01 * p['k'] * r / (r @ r) ** (2.01/2 + 1)

def rk4(F, s, h, N):
    rhs = lambda s: np.concatenate([s[2:], F(s[:2])])
    out = np.zeros((N, 4)); out[0] = s
    for i in range(1, N):
        k1 = rhs(out[i-1])
        k2 = rhs(out[i-1] + 0.5*h*k1)
        k3 = rhs(out[i-1] + 0.5*h*k2)
        k4 = rhs(out[i-1] + h*k3)
        out[i] = out[i-1] + (h/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)
    return out

# Demo: Kepler orbit
s0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.95])

import numpy as np

# Force factory and RK4 integrator (same as the first tab) so this runs standalone
def make_F(potential, **p):
    if potential == 'kepler':
        return lambda r: -p['k'] * r / (r @ r) ** 1.5
    if potential == 'harmonic':
        return lambda r: -p['K'] * r
    if potential == 'yukawa':
        def F_y(r):
            rmag = np.sqrt(r @ r)
            return -p['k'] * r * np.exp(-rmag/p['r0']) * (
                1/rmag**3 + 1/(p['r0']*rmag**2))
        return F_y
    if potential == 'power101':
        return lambda r: -1.01 * p['k'] * r / (r @ r) ** (2.01/2 + 1)

def rk4(F, s, h, N):
    rhs = lambda s: np.concatenate([s[2:], F(s[:2])])
    out = np.zeros((N, 4)); out[0] = s
    for i in range(1, N):
        k1 = rhs(out[i-1]); k2 = rhs(out[i-1] + 0.5*h*k1)
        k3 = rhs(out[i-1] + 0.5*h*k2); k4 = rhs(out[i-1] + h*k3)
        out[i] = out[i-1] + (h/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)
    return out

# Quantify precession: angle between successive perihelia.
def perihelion_angles(traj):
    r = np.sqrt(traj[:,0]**2 + traj[:,1]**2)
    # local minima of r
    mins = (r[1:-1] < r[:-2]) & (r[1:-1] < r[2:])
    idx = np.where(mins)[0] + 1
    angles = np.arctan2(traj[idx, 1], traj[idx, 0])
    return idx, angles

s0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.95])
h, N = 0.005, 12000

for label, pot, params in [
    ('Kepler',     'kepler',   {'k': 1.0}),
    ('Harmonic',   'harmonic', {'K': 1.0}),
    ('Yukawa',     'yukawa',   {'k': 1.0, 'r0': 3}),
    ('Power-1.01', 'power101', {'k': 1.0})]:
    traj = rk4(make_F(pot, **params), s0, h, N)
    _, ang = perihelion_angles(traj)
    if len(ang) >= 2:
        # Convert successive angle differences mod 2π
        d = np.diff(np.unwrap(ang))
        precess_per_orbit = (d.mean() - 2*np.pi if d.mean() > np.pi else d.mean()) * 180/np.pi
        print(f'{label:12s}: {len(ang)} perihelia, precession ≈ {precess_per_orbit:+.2f}° / orbit')
    else:
        print(f'{label:12s}: only {len(ang)} perihelion seen — extend integration')

Chapter 8 Wrap-Up

Chapter 8 in One Paragraph.

We started with two coupled gravitational bodies (§ 8.1) and cut the problem in half via center-of-mass and relative coordinates. Conservation laws (§ 8.2) — energy and angular momentum — turned out to be both physical theorems and numerical sanity checks. Polar coordinates (§ 8.3) revealed the angular-momentum conservation as the φ-equation of motion and gave us a 1-D radial equation with a centrifugal term. The effective potential (§ 8.4) collapsed the orbit-classification problem into reading horizontal lines on a 1-D plot. For the special Kepler case (§ 8.5) we solved the trajectory integral analytically and recovered all three of Kepler's empirical laws as theorems of Newton's gravity. The numerical integrator (§ 8.6) gave us a reliable computational tool that matches Section 8.5's analytical answers and works for any potential. And finally (§ 8.7) we showed that nature is rich beyond the Kepler problem — Bertrand's theorem says only \( -1/\rho \) and \( \rho^2 \) close; everything else precesses, and that precession is the doorway to general relativity, molecular physics, nuclear physics, and beyond.
