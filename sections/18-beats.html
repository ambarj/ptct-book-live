Beats: Near-Resonance Behavior

What happens when you add two waves at slightly different frequencies? Something new: the sum doesn't oscillate at a single amplitude. Instead, the amplitude itself slowly rises and falls — a phenomenon called beats. If you've ever heard two guitar strings slightly out of tune, that pulsing "wah-wah-wah" is beats. Piano tuners use them every day: when the beats slow down, the strings are getting closer to the same pitch. When the beats vanish, the tuning is perfect.

Learning Objectives
  • Derive the beat frequency formula from the product-to-sum identity
  • Distinguish the carrier frequency from the envelope frequency
  • Explain why the beat frequency is twice the envelope frequency
  • Predict the beat frequency and period from two given frequencies

The product-to-sum identity:

$$ \cos(\omega_1 t) + \cos(\omega_2 t) = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}\,t\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{2}\,t\right) $$

The sum of two cosines becomes a product of a fast oscillation at the average frequency \( \bar{\omega} = (\omega_1 + \omega_2)/2 \) and a slow modulation at half the difference \( \Delta\omega/2 = (\omega_1 - \omega_2)/2 \). Since the ear perceives two amplitude peaks per envelope cycle, the beat frequency is \( f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2| \).

Hearing (and Seeing) Beats

Bring the two frequencies close together and watch the beat pattern emerge. The yellow sum wave swells and fades rhythmically. The red dashed envelope shows the amplitude modulation. When \( f_1 = f_2 \) exactly, the beats disappear and you're back to ordinary constructive interference.

Interactive: Beat Generator
Key Insight

Start with f1 = 5.0 Hz and f2 = 5.5 Hz. The beat frequency is \( |5.0 - 5.5| = 0.5 \) Hz, so the amplitude swells once every 2 seconds. Now bring f2 closer to 5.0 Hz: the beats slow down. At f2 = 5.1 Hz, one beat takes 10 seconds. At f2 = 5.0 Hz exactly, the beats are infinitely slow — they vanish, and you get steady constructive interference. This is why piano tuners listen for the beat rate to approach zero.

Explanation
\( T_{\text{beat}} = 1/f_{\text{beat}} = 1/|5.0 - 5.5| = 1/0.5 = 2 \) seconds. Look at the plot: the envelope peaks are spaced 2 seconds apart. Don't confuse the beat period with the carrier period (\( T_{\text{carrier}} \approx 1/5.25 \approx 0.19 \) s).
Set A1 = 1.5 and A2 = 0.5. Does the sum ever reach zero amplitude?
Explanation
With unequal amplitudes, complete cancellation never occurs. The instantaneous amplitude oscillates between \( A_1 + A_2 = 2.0 \) and \( |A_1 - A_2| = 1.0 \) — the signal fades but never fully dies. (The individual oscillation does cross zero, but the envelope never reaches zero.) Complete beats (full fade-out) only happen when \( A_1 = A_2 \).

Anatomy of a Beat

The product-to-sum identity reveals the two components hidden inside a beat pattern:

  • Carrier (cyan): the fast oscillation at the average frequency \( \bar{f} = (f_1 + f_2)/2 \). This is the pitch you hear.
  • Envelope (red): the slow modulation at \( f_{\text{env}} = |f_1 - f_2|/2 \). This controls the loudness.

The beat frequency is twice the envelope frequency because your ear hears two loudness peaks per envelope cycle — one on the positive swing and one on the negative.

Interactive: Anatomy of a Beat
Key Insight

The red × markers on the right panel show the beat nodes — instants where the envelope passes through zero and the sum momentarily vanishes. Count the distance between successive nodes: that's half the beat period, because the envelope goes positive-zero-negative-zero in one full cycle. This is why \( f_{\text{beat}} = 2 \times f_{\text{envelope}} = |f_1 - f_2| \). Try widening the frequency gap and watch the nodes crowd together.

Why is the beat frequency twice the envelope frequency?
Explanation
The envelope oscillates as \( \cos(\Delta\omega\, t/2) \), going from +1 to −1 and back. But loudness depends on the absolute value of the envelope: both the positive peak and the negative peak produce a loud moment. So you hear two loudness peaks per envelope cycle: \( f_{\text{beat}} = 2 \times f_{\text{envelope}} = |f_1 - f_2| \).
Which statements about beats are correct? (Select all that apply)
Explanation
  • True: Beats arise from adding two signals — pure superposition. No nonlinearity is needed.
  • False: The carrier is the average \( (f_1+f_2)/2 \), not the difference. The difference gives the beat frequency.
  • True: As \( f_1 \to f_2 \), \( f_{\text{beat}} \to 0 \) and the beat period \( \to \infty \). In the limit, both waves are identical and the sum is just a steady wave at double amplitude.
  • False: Beats occur in any wave system. In optics, beating between two laser frequencies produces measurable intensity modulation (heterodyne detection). Radio receivers use the same principle.

A piano tuner strikes a 440 Hz tuning fork and a piano string simultaneously. She hears 3 beats per second. What are the possible frequencies of the string?
Explanation
3 beats per second means \( |f_{\text{string}} - 440| = 3 \), so \( f_{\text{string}} = 437 \) or \( 443 \) Hz. The beat frequency tells you how far off the string is, but not which direction. To determine if it's sharp or flat, the tuner adds a small piece of tape to the fork (lowering its frequency slightly): if the beats speed up, the string is flat; if they slow down, it's sharp.
Which are true about the product-to-sum decomposition? (Select all that apply)
Explanation
  • False: The product-to-sum identity \( \cos\alpha + \cos\beta = 2\cos\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\cos\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2} \) is exact for equal amplitudes. For unequal amplitudes, you can still decompose but the algebra is different.
  • True: \( \bar{f} = (f_1+f_2)/2 \) is the arithmetic mean, which always lies between \( f_1 \) and \( f_2 \).
  • False: This is a linear operation — no new frequencies are created. The carrier and envelope frequencies are just different combinations of the original two. (New frequencies appear only in nonlinear systems.)
  • True: It's a purely mathematical identity, valid for any two cosines regardless of their frequencies.

Python Code

Here's the Python equivalent for beat simulations and analysis.

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

f1, f2 = 5.0, 5.5  # Hz
omega1, omega2 = 2*np.pi*f1, 2*np.pi*f2

f_beat = abs(f1 - f2)
T_beat = 1 / f_beat
t = np.linspace(0, 4*T_beat, 4000)  # show 4 beat cycles

x1 = np.cos(omega1 * t)
x2 = np.cos(omega2 * t)
x_sum = x1 + x2

# Envelope: 2|cos(Δω·t/2)|
envelope = 2 * np.abs(np.cos((omega1-omega2)*t/2))

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x_sum, name='Sum',
    line=dict(color='#ffff00', width=1.5)))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=envelope, name='Envelope',
    line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', width=2, dash='dash')))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=-envelope, showlegend=False,
    line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', width=2, dash='dash')))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title=f'Beats: f₁={f1} Hz, f₂={f2} Hz → f_beat={f_beat} Hz',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='x(t)', range=[-2.5, 2.5], gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
print(f"f_beat = {f_beat} Hz, T_beat = {T_beat} s")
print(f"f_carrier = {(f1+f2)/2} Hz")
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

f1, f2 = 5.0, 5.5  # Hz
omega1, omega2 = 2*np.pi*f1, 2*np.pi*f2
omega_avg = (omega1 + omega2) / 2
omega_diff = (omega1 - omega2) / 2

t = np.linspace(0, 8, 4000)

# Product-to-sum: cos(ω₁t) + cos(ω₂t) = 2cos(ω_avg·t)cos(Δω·t/2)
carrier = np.cos(omega_avg * t)
envelope = np.cos(omega_diff * t)
product = 2 * carrier * envelope
direct_sum = np.cos(omega1*t) + np.cos(omega2*t)

# Verify they match
print(f"Max difference: {np.max(np.abs(product - direct_sum)):.2e}")

fig = make_subplots(rows=2, cols=1, shared_xaxes=True,
    subplot_titles=['Sum (carrier × envelope)',
                    'Separated Components'])

fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=direct_sum, name='Sum',
    line=dict(color='#ffff00', width=1.5)), row=1, col=1)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=2*envelope, name='Envelope',
    line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', width=2, dash='dash')), row=1, col=1)

fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=carrier, name='Carrier',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=1.5)), row=2, col=1)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=envelope, name='Envelope (norm)',
    line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', width=2.5)), row=2, col=1)

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark', height=600)
fig.update_xaxes(title='Time (s)', gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
    zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080')
fig.update_yaxes(gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
    zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080')
fig.show()
