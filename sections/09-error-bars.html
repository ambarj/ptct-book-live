From Samples to Populations: Error Bars

You measure the acceleration due to gravity and get \( g = 9.7 \; \text{m/s}^2 \). Is that good? Bad? Meaningless — without an error bar, there's no way to tell. An error bar transforms a number into a statement about the world: "my measurement says \( g \) is probably between 9.5 and 9.9." This section teaches you how to compute that range from your data, honestly and correctly.

Learning Objectives
  • Compute the sample mean \( \bar{X} \) and sample standard deviation \( s \) from data
  • Understand why we divide by \( N-1 \) instead of \( N \) (Bessel's correction)
  • Calculate the standard error: \( s/\sqrt{N-1} \)
  • Use error bars to judge whether two measurements agree

Building an Error Bar from Scratch

Imagine measuring \( g \) by timing a falling ball. Each measurement gives a slightly different answer because of reaction time, air currents, and other random noise. The explorer below simulates this: it generates \( N \) "measurements" of \( g = 9.8 \; \text{m/s}^2 \) with controllable noise, then computes the error bar. Watch how the yellow band shrinks as you collect more data.

Interactive: Error Bar Builder

The practical formula that every physicist uses:

\[ \text{Result} = \bar{X} \pm \frac{s}{\sqrt{N-1}} \]

where \( \bar{X} \) is the sample mean, \( s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum(x_i - \bar{X})^2} \) is the sample standard deviation, and \( N \) is the number of measurements. The quantity \( s/\sqrt{N-1} \) is the standard error of the mean — it tells you how precisely you've pinned down the true value.

Key Insight

The yellow band is the error bar: the region \( \bar{X} \pm s/\sqrt{N-1} \). The magenta line is the true value \( g = 9.8 \). Click "New Measurements" several times — the true value falls inside the error bar roughly 68% of the time. That's what a "one-sigma" error bar means: it captures the truth about two-thirds of the time.

  • Halve σ: Since \( s \propto \sigma \), halving the noise directly halves the error. Yes!

Why N−1? Bessel's Correction

Here's a subtle trap. When you compute the sample variance \( s^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum(x_i - \bar{X})^2 \), you're dividing by \( N \). But this systematically underestimates the true variance. Why? Because you're measuring deviations from \( \bar{X} \) (your estimate) instead of from \( \mu \) (the true mean) — and \( \bar{X} \) is always closer to the data than \( \mu \) is. The fix is simple: divide by \( N-1 \) instead of \( N \).

Interactive: Bessel's Correction

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \bar{X})^2 = \frac{N}{N-1}\,s^2 \]

Key Insight

The cyan histogram (dividing by \( N \)) is systematically shifted left — it underestimates the true variance \( \sigma^2 = 4 \). The green histogram (dividing by \( N-1 \)) is centered correctly on the true value. The bias is worst at small \( N \): try N=2 vs N=50 to see how the correction becomes negligible for large samples.

Increase N from 3 to 50. What happens to the difference between the biased and unbiased estimates?
Explanation
At N=50, the correction factor is \( N/(N-1) = 50/49 \approx 1.02 \) — a mere 2% difference. The histograms nearly overlap. Bessel's correction matters most for small samples. For N > 30, the difference is usually negligible in practice. But for N=2 or N=3, it's crucial.

Do These Measurements Agree?

Two labs both measure the speed of light. Lab A gets \( 2.998 \times 10^8 \pm 0.003 \) m/s; Lab B gets \( 2.994 \times 10^8 \pm 0.004 \) m/s. Do they agree? The physicist's quick heuristic: if the error bars overlap, the measurements are consistent. If they don't, something interesting might be going on. The explorer below lets you test this intuition.

Interactive: Do These Measurements Agree?
Key Insight

When offset = 0, both labs measure the same quantity — their error bars should usually overlap. Increase the offset and watch the error bars separate. The combined uncertainty in the difference is \( \sqrt{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2} \). Physicists consider measurements "significantly discrepant" when they differ by more than about 2–3 combined uncertainties.

From Q8: the difference is \( |10.2 - 9.5| = 0.7 \), and the combined uncertainty is 0.5. The ratio 0.7/0.5 = 1.4. In physics, measurements are considered "significantly discrepant" when this ratio exceeds ~2–3. Are these measurements consistent?
Explanation
A 1.4σ discrepancy occurs by chance about 16% of the time — unremarkable. In particle physics, the convention is: 2σ is "interesting," 3σ is "evidence," and 5σ is "discovery." A 1.4σ difference is well within the range of normal statistical fluctuation. The measurements are consistent.

Python Code Examples

Here's the Python code for computing error bars and comparing measurements.

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Simulate N measurements of g with noise
true_g = 9.8
sigma = 0.5     # measurement noise
N = 20

measurements = np.random.normal(true_g, sigma, N)
x_bar = np.mean(measurements)
s = np.std(measurements, ddof=0)      # biased sample std
error = s / np.sqrt(N - 1)            # standard error of mean

print(f"Sample mean:  X̄ = {x_bar:.3f} m/s²")
print(f"Sample std:    s = {s:.3f}")
print(f"Error bar:     s/√(N-1) = {error:.3f}")
print(f"Result:        g = {x_bar:.2f} ± {error:.2f} m/s²")

# Plot measurements with error bar
fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=list(range(1, N+1)), y=measurements,
    mode='markers', name='Measurements',
    marker=dict(color='#00f3ff', size=8)))

# Mean line and error band
fig.add_hline(y=x_bar, line_dash='dash',
    line_color='#ffff00', annotation_text=f'X̄={x_bar:.2f}')
fig.add_hrect(y0=x_bar-error, y1=x_bar+error,
    fillcolor='rgba(255,255,0,0.1)', line_width=0)
fig.add_hline(y=true_g, line_dash='dot',
    line_color='#ff00ff', annotation_text='True g')

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    title=f'g = {x_bar:.2f} ± {error:.2f} m/s²',
    xaxis_title='Measurement #', yaxis_title='g (m/s²)')
fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np

# Verify Bessel's correction with simulation
N = 5
n_experiments = 10000
true_sigma_sq = 4.0   # σ² for Gaussian(μ=10, σ=2)

biased = []
unbiased = []
for _ in range(n_experiments):
    data = np.random.normal(10, 2, N)
    biased.append(np.var(data, ddof=0))    # ÷N
    unbiased.append(np.var(data, ddof=1))  # ÷(N-1)

print(f"True σ²          = {true_sigma_sq}")
print(f"Mean biased  (÷N)   = {np.mean(biased):.3f}")
print(f"Mean unbiased (÷N-1) = {np.mean(unbiased):.3f}")
print(f"Theory for biased    = {true_sigma_sq * (N-1)/N:.3f}")
print()
print("Bessel's correction works: ÷(N-1) gives the right answer!")
print(f"Bias at N={N}: {(1 - (N-1)/N)*100:.0f}% underestimate")
