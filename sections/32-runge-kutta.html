A Better Algorithm: The Midpoint Method

Euler’s method has a fundamental flaw: it checks the slope at the start of each step and blindly follows it. By the time you arrive at the next point, the slope has changed — and you’ve been walking in the wrong direction the whole time.

Here’s a deceptively simple fix: take a trial half-step, check the slope there, then go back and use that midpoint slope for the full step. This is called the midpoint method (also known as RK2 — second-order Runge-Kutta). It’s like checking your compass halfway through instead of just at the start. That one extra look cuts the error dramatically.

Learning Objectives
  • Understand why checking the slope at the midpoint beats Euler
  • Use the RK2 recipe as a “black box” to solve ODEs more accurately
  • Read convergence plots: identify O(h) vs O(h²) from log-log slopes
  • Appreciate that better algorithms exist — we’ll build on this in Chapter 4

The Midpoint Method (RK2) — a recipe you can use right now:

$$ \text{1. Compute the slope at the start:} \quad \mathbf{k}_1 = f(\mathbf{y}_n,\, t_n) $$ $$ \text{2. Take a trial half-step to the midpoint:} \quad \mathbf{y}_{\text{mid}} = \mathbf{y}_n + \tfrac{h}{2}\,\mathbf{k}_1 $$ $$ \text{3. Compute a better slope at the midpoint:} \quad \mathbf{k}_2 = f(\mathbf{y}_{\text{mid}},\, t_n + \tfrac{h}{2}) $$ $$ \text{4. Use the better slope for the full step:} \quad \mathbf{y}_{n+1} = \mathbf{y}_n + h\,\mathbf{k}_2 $$

That’s it — two slope evaluations instead of one. The payoff is big: this method is second-order accurate, which means halving your step size cuts the error by a factor of 4 (not just 2, as with Euler). We’ll see exactly what that means in Explorer 2 below.

The Midpoint Intuition

Think of driving on a curved road. Euler looks at the road direction at your current position and drives straight for a while. The midpoint method drives half the distance, looks at the road direction there, then goes back and drives the whole distance using that mid-road direction. Since the midpoint slope better represents the curve’s average direction, you stay on the road much more faithfully.

Convergence: How Fast Does Error Shrink?

How do we measure whether a method is first-order or second-order? The left panel plots error vs step size on a log-log scale. On this kind of plot, a power law error ∝ hp shows up as a straight line whose slope equals p. So Euler (first-order, p = 1) gives a line with slope 1, and RK2 (second-order, p = 2) gives a steeper line with slope 2. The dashed guidelines are there to help you eyeball the slopes.

What does this mean in practice? If you halve h, Euler’s error drops by a factor of 2 (because 21 = 2). But RK2’s error drops by a factor of 4 (because 22 = 4). The right panel shows this directly: it computes the actual improvement you get by halving h for each method.

On the log-log convergence plot, what do the slopes of the data lines tell you?
Explanation: If error scales as hp, then on a log-log plot (where both axes use logarithmic spacing), this relationship becomes a straight line with slope p. So you can literally read the convergence order off the graph: Euler’s line has slope 1 (first-order), RK2’s line has slope 2 (second-order). Steeper slope = faster error reduction as you shrink h.
Increase the number of periods to 8. What happens to the convergence lines on the log-log plot?
Explanation: More periods means more time for errors to accumulate, so the error at each h increases (lines shift up). But the order (slope) doesn’t change — Euler is still O(h), RK2 is still O(h²). The convergence order is a property of the algorithm, not the problem duration.

The Energy Test

Our oscillator has no friction, so its total energy should stay constant forever. That’s a great reality check for any numerical method: does it conserve energy?

The left panel plots E(t)/E(0) — the ratio of the numerical energy at time t to the initial energy. A perfect method would keep this flat at 1.0. Euler’s curve climbs steadily upward: the algorithm is pumping fake energy into the system, step after step. RK2 isn’t perfect either, but its drift is tiny by comparison.

The right panel shows what this energy error looks like in practice: Euler’s oscillation amplitude visibly grows over time, while RK2 stays close to the exact solution. Crank up the simulation time to 50 s and the difference becomes dramatic.

The bottom line: RK2 gives you much better accuracy for the same amount of computation.
What Comes Next: Even Better Methods

The midpoint method is just the beginning. It belongs to a family of methods called Runge-Kutta, named after the mathematicians who invented them around 1900. In Chapter 4, we’ll code Euler and RK2 from scratch, then meet the star of the family: RK4. That method checks the slope four times per step and is fourth-order accurate — halving h cuts the error by a factor of 16. RK4 is the workhorse of computational physics, and you’ll build it yourself.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

def rk2_step(f, y, t, h):
    """One step of RK2 (midpoint method)."""
    k1 = f(y, t)                        # slope at start
    y_mid = y + 0.5 * h * k1            # trial half-step
    k2 = f(y_mid, t + 0.5 * h)          # slope at midpoint
    return y + h * k2                   # full step with better slope

def solve_ode(f, y0, t_span, h):
    """Solve dy/dt = f(y,t) using the midpoint method."""
    t = np.arange(t_span[0], t_span[1] + h/2, h)
    y = np.zeros((len(t), len(y0)))
    y[0] = y0
    for i in range(len(t) - 1):
        y[i+1] = rk2_step(f, y[i], t[i], h)
    return t, y

# SHO: x'' + omega0^2 x = 0  ->  [dx/dt, dv/dt] = [v, -omega0^2 x]
omega0 = 2.0
f = lambda y, t: np.array([y[1], -omega0**2 * y[0]])

t, y = solve_ode(f, y0=np.array([1.0, 0.0]),
                 t_span=(0, 10), h=0.20)

t_exact = np.linspace(0, 10, 500)
x_exact = np.cos(omega0 * t_exact)

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t_exact, y=x_exact, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00ff9f', width=2), name='Exact'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=y[:, 0], mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2), name='RK2 h=0.20'))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title='RK2 vs Exact: Simple Harmonic Oscillator',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', range=[0, 10],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='x(t)', range=[-1.5, 1.5],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

omega0 = 2.0
f = lambda y, t: np.array([y[1], -omega0**2 * y[0]])

def euler(f, y0, t_span, h):
    t = np.arange(t_span[0], t_span[1]+h/2, h)
    y = np.zeros((len(t), len(y0))); y[0] = y0
    for i in range(len(t)-1):
        y[i+1] = y[i] + h * f(y[i], t[i])
    return t, y

def rk2(f, y0, t_span, h):
    t = np.arange(t_span[0], t_span[1]+h/2, h)
    y = np.zeros((len(t), len(y0))); y[0] = y0
    for i in range(len(t)-1):
        k1 = f(y[i], t[i])
        k2 = f(y[i] + 0.5*h*k1, t[i] + 0.5*h)
        y[i+1] = y[i] + h * k2
    return t, y

# Convergence analysis: sweep h values
h_values = np.logspace(-2.5, -0.3, 30)
t_max = 3 * 2*np.pi/omega0  # 3 periods
y0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
x_exact_final = np.cos(omega0 * t_max)

methods = {'Euler': euler, 'RK2': rk2}
colors = {'Euler': '#ff6b6b', 'RK2': '#00f3ff'}

fig = go.Figure()
for name, method in methods.items():
    errors = []
    for h in h_values:
        t, y = method(f, y0, (0, t_max), h)
        errors.append(abs(y[-1, 0] - x_exact_final))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=h_values, y=errors,
        mode='lines+markers', line=dict(color=colors[name], width=2),
        marker=dict(size=4), name=name))

# Reference slopes
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0.01, 0.5], y=[0.01, 0.5],
    mode='lines', line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', dash='dot', width=1),
    name='slope 1', showlegend=True))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0.01, 0.5], y=[0.01**2, 0.5**2],
    mode='lines', line=dict(color='#00f3ff', dash='dot', width=1),
    name='slope 2', showlegend=True))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title='Convergence: Euler O(h) vs RK2 O(h²)',
    xaxis=dict(title='h', type='log',
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='|Error|', type='log',
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
