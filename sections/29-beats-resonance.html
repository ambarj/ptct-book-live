Beats and Near-Resonance

What happens when the driving frequency is close to the natural frequency but not exactly equal? You don't get steady resonance — you get something stranger. The amplitude rises and falls in a slow, periodic rhythm called beats. The system alternates between moments of strong response (constructive interference) and near-silence (destructive interference).

Beats are the bridge between "nothing special" (frequencies far apart) and resonance (frequencies equal). As you tune \( \omega_d \) closer to \( \omega_0 \), the beats slow down, the peak amplitude grows, and you slide smoothly toward the resonance catastrophe.

Learning Objectives
  • Explain why near-resonant driving produces amplitude modulation (beats)
  • Calculate the beat frequency \( f_{\text{beat}} = |\omega_0 - \omega_d| / (2\pi) \) and beat period
  • Use the product-to-sum identity to decompose beats into carrier and envelope
  • Describe how beats transition into resonance as \( \omega_d \rightarrow \omega_0 \)

Beats from the driven oscillator (undamped, \( x(0) = 0, v(0) = 0 \)):

$$ x(t) = \frac{F_0/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2}\bigl[\cos(\omega_d t) - \cos(\omega_0 t)\bigr] $$

There's a trig identity that lets us rewrite a difference of two cosines as a product of two sines (you can verify it by expanding the right side). Applying it here separates the motion into a slow part and a fast part:

$$ x(t) = \frac{2(F_0/m)}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2}\;\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\Delta\omega}{2}\,t\Bigr)\;\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\omega_0 + \omega_d}{2}\,t\Bigr) $$

Slow envelope: \( \sin(\Delta\omega\, t/2) \) with beat period \( T_{\text{beat}} = 2\pi / \Delta\omega \)
Fast carrier: \( \sin(\bar\omega\, t) \) at the average frequency \( \bar\omega = (\omega_0 + \omega_d)/2 \)

Explorer 1: Beat Visualizer
Key Insight

Beats are simply two close frequencies interfering. The sum \( \cos(\omega_d t) - \cos(\omega_0 t) \) is exactly the same mathematics as two-wave interference from Chapter 2 — but now one frequency is the drive and the other is the system's natural response. The "slow" factor \( \sin(\Delta\omega\, t/2) \) is the envelope, and the "fast" factor \( \sin(\bar\omega\, t) \) is the carrier. When you add damping, the natural-frequency component decays and only the driving frequency survives — the beats fade to a steady state.

The Road to Resonance

Slowly drag \( \omega_d \) toward \( \omega_0 = 3.0 \) and watch the transformation. The beats get slower (longer \( T_{\text{beat}} \)), taller (larger peak amplitude), and eventually — at exact resonance — the beat period becomes infinite and the amplitude grows without bound. The right panel shows how the peak envelope height diverges as \( \Delta\omega \rightarrow 0 \).

Explorer 2: Approaching Resonance
Key Insight

At exact resonance (\( \omega_d = \omega_0 \)), the undamped solution becomes \( x(t) = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}\, t \sin(\omega_0 t) \). The factor of \( t \) means the amplitude grows linearly with time — the system absorbs energy every cycle and never gives it back. This is the limiting case of beats: an infinitely slow beat with ever-increasing peaks. In real systems, damping prevents this, capping the amplitude at \( A_{\text{peak}} = F_0 / (2m\gamma\omega_0) \).

Explorer 3: Beat Frequency Challenge
Key Insight

Measuring beat frequency is a real experimental technique. Piano tuners listen for beats between a tuning fork and a piano string. When the beats slow to zero, the string is in tune. The same principle is used in heterodyne receivers, frequency counters, and laser stabilization. Every time you hear a "wah-wah-wah" between two notes, you're hearing beats — and the beat rate tells you how far apart the frequencies are.

Code It Yourself

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

omega0, F0m = 3.0, 1.0
wd = 2.7  # close to omega0
dw = omega0 - wd

t = np.linspace(0, 80, 2000)
# Exact undamped solution
x = F0m / (omega0**2 - wd**2) * (np.cos(wd*t) - np.cos(omega0*t))

# Envelope: ±2F0m / |ω₀²-ω_d²| × |sin(Δω t/2)|
env = 2*F0m / abs(omega0**2 - wd**2) * np.abs(np.sin(dw*t/2))

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=env, name='Envelope',
    line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', dash='dash', width=1.5)))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=-env, showlegend=False,
    line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', dash='dash', width=1.5)))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x, name='x(t)',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=1.5)))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title=f'Beats: ω₀={omega0}, ω_d={wd}, Δω={dw:.2f}',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', range=[0, 80],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='x(t)', range=[-1.5, 1.5],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

omega0, F0m = 3.0, 1.0
t = np.linspace(0, 60, 2000)

fig = go.Figure()
for wd, color in [(2.5, '#00f3ff'), (2.8, '#00ff9f'),
                   (2.95, '#ffff00'), (2.99, '#ff6b6b')]:
    dw = omega0 - wd
    x = F0m / (omega0**2 - wd**2) * (np.cos(wd*t) - np.cos(omega0*t))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x,
        name=f'ω_d={wd} (Δω={dw:.2f})',
        line=dict(color=color, width=1.5)))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title='Approaching Resonance: Beats Slow and Grow',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', range=[0, 60],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='x(t)',
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
