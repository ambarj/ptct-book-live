3.2 The Math Behind the Motion

💡
Learning Objectives:
  • Connect the observation \( x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \) to the differential equation \( \ddot{x} = -\omega^2 x \)
  • Understand how initial conditions \( x_0, v_0 \) uniquely determine \( A \) and \( \varphi \)
  • Verify that the cosine solution actually satisfies the ODE by computing derivatives

In the previous section, you explored how three parameters — amplitude \( A \), angular frequency \( \omega \), and phase \( \varphi \) — describe any simple harmonic motion. But why does nature choose cosines? The answer lies in a single, elegant equation that governs all oscillatory systems:

The Simple Harmonic Oscillator Equation:

$$ \ddot{x} = -\omega^2 x $$

In words: the acceleration is proportional to displacement, and always points back toward equilibrium.

This one equation — just five symbols — contains everything. The minus sign guarantees a restoring force (always pulling back toward center). The \( \omega^2 \) sets the clock speed. And the acceleration depends on \( x \) itself, not on \( x^2 \) or \( x^3 \) — this “proportional” relationship is what makes the motion perfectly sinusoidal. Let’s see this connection come alive.

The Equation in Action

If \( x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \), then by calculus:

$$ v(t) = \dot{x} = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \varphi) $$ $$ a(t) = \ddot{x} = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \varphi) = -\omega^2 x(t) $$

That last line is the magic — the second derivative gives back the original function times \( -\omega^2 \). The cosine solution automatically satisfies the ODE. Watch this in the explorer below: the left panel shows all three quantities in time, while the right panel reveals the linear relationship between \( a \) and \( x \).

Look at the \( a \) vs \( x \) plot (right panel). Which statements are true? (Select all that apply)
Explanation
  • Straight line through origin: True — \( a = -\omega^2 x \) is a linear equation with zero intercept.
  • Slope depends on A: False — the slope is \( -\omega^2 \), independent of amplitude. Changing \( A \) only changes how far along the line you travel.
  • Steeper with larger \( \omega \): True — slope = \( -\omega^2 \), so doubling \( \omega \) quadruples the magnitude of the slope.
  • Phase changes slope: False — phase only shifts the starting point on the line, not the line itself.

From Initial Conditions to Motion

The general solution \( x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \) has two free constants: the amplitude \( A \) and the phase \( \varphi \). These aren't chosen by the equation — they're determined by how the oscillation started. If you know the initial position \( x_0 = x(0) \) and initial velocity \( v_0 = v(0) \), you can find \( A \) and \( \varphi \):

Initial Condition Formulas:

$$ A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + \frac{v_0^2}{\omega^2}} \qquad \varphi = \arctan\!\left(\frac{-v_0}{\omega\, x_0}\right) $$

The system frequency \( \omega \) is fixed by the physics (spring constant, mass, etc.). Only \( A \) and \( \varphi \) depend on how you start it.

Explorer 2: Initial Condition Explorer
Key Insight

The phasor diagram (right) shows why the formula works: the amplitude \( A \) is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs \( x_0 \) and \( v_0/\omega \). That's just the Pythagorean theorem! The phase angle \( \varphi \) is the angle this phasor makes with the positive x-axis.

⚠️
Common Mistake:

Students often think "released from rest" means \( \varphi = 0 \). That's only true if released from a positive displacement. If \( x_0 = -2, v_0 = 0 \), then \( \varphi = \pi \) (180°). Try it in the explorer!

Watching the Verification

Here's the most satisfying part of the SHO: we can watch the differential equation being satisfied at every instant. The three panels below show position, velocity, and acceleration. In the bottom panel, the yellow curve is \( a(t) = \ddot{x} \) computed by differentiating twice, while the green dashed curve is \( -\omega^2 x(t) \). They overlap perfectly — that's what "satisfies the ODE" means.

Explorer 3: Solution Verifier
Key Insight

The three quantities form a phase cascade: velocity is 90° behind position, and acceleration is 90° behind velocity (i.e., 180° behind position). That's why when \( x \) is at its peak, \( a \) is at its most negative — the restoring force is strongest when you're farthest from equilibrium.

Use the Play button and watch the red dot. By how many degrees does the velocity peak lag behind the position peak? (Answer in degrees) °
Explanation
Velocity lags position by exactly 90°. Since \( v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \varphi) = A\omega\cos(\omega t + \varphi + \pi/2) \), the velocity is a cosine shifted by \( \pi/2 = 90° \). When position peaks, velocity is zero; when velocity peaks, position is at zero crossing.
Set \( \omega = 4.0 \) and \( A = 1.0 \). The maximum velocity is \( A\omega = 4.0 \) and the maximum acceleration is \( A\omega^2 = 16.0 \). Now set \( \omega = 1.0 \). What are the new maximum velocity and acceleration?
Explanation
With \( A = 1.0 \) and \( \omega = 1.0 \): \( v_{\text{max}} = A\omega = 1.0 \times 1.0 = 1.0 \) and \( a_{\text{max}} = A\omega^2 = 1.0 \times 1.0 = 1.0 \). Reducing \( \omega \) from 4 to 1 reduced the max velocity by a factor of 4 and the max acceleration by a factor of 16. Frequency has a dramatic effect on how violently something oscillates!

Multiple Representations

The SHO solution can be written in several equivalent forms:

Three equivalent ways to write the same solution:

$$ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \qquad \text{(amplitude-phase form — most intuitive)} $$ $$ x(t) = B\cos(\omega t) + C\sin(\omega t) \qquad \text{(trig form — easiest for initial conditions)} $$ $$ x(t) = F\sin(\omega t + \psi) \qquad \text{(sine-phase form — just a shifted cosine)} $$

These are all the same motion, just written differently. The trig form is handy because plugging in t = 0 immediately gives \( B = x_0 \) and \( C = v_0/\omega \). (In more advanced courses you’ll meet a fourth form using complex exponentials — powerful but not needed here.)

Code It Yourself

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

# SHO parameters
A, omega, phi = 2.0, 3.0, np.pi/4
t = np.linspace(0, 4*np.pi/omega, 500)

# Position, velocity, acceleration
x = A * np.cos(omega * t + phi)
v = -A * omega * np.sin(omega * t + phi)
a = -A * omega**2 * np.cos(omega * t + phi)

# Verify the ODE: a should equal -omega^2 * x
print(f"Max |a + omega^2 * x| = {np.max(np.abs(a + omega**2 * x)):.2e}")
# Should be ~0 (machine precision)

# Three-panel plot
fig = make_subplots(rows=3, cols=1, shared_xaxes=True,
                    subplot_titles=['Position x(t)', 'Velocity v(t)',
                                    'Acceleration a(t) vs -ω²x(t)'])

fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x, line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2),
                          name='x(t)'), row=1, col=1)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=v, line=dict(color='#ff00ff', width=2),
                          name='v(t)'), row=2, col=1)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=a, line=dict(color='#ffff00', width=2),
                          name='a(t)'), row=3, col=1)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=-omega**2 * x,
                          line=dict(color='#00ff9f', width=2, dash='dash'),

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark', height=600)
for i in range(1, 4):
    fig.update_xaxes(gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', row=i, col=1)
    fig.update_yaxes(gridcolor='#2a2f4a', zerolinecolor='#808080', row=i, col=1)

fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

def solve_sho(x0, v0, omega, t):
    """Solve SHO from initial conditions."""
    A = np.sqrt(x0**2 + (v0/omega)**2)
    phi = np.arctan2(-v0/omega, x0)
    x = A * np.cos(omega * t + phi)
    v = -A * omega * np.sin(omega * t + phi)
    return x, v, A, phi

# Example: released from x0=1 with velocity v0=3
omega = 2.0
x0, v0 = 1.0, 3.0
t = np.linspace(0, 10, 500)

x, v, A, phi = solve_sho(x0, v0, omega, t)
print(f"A = {A:.3f}, phi = {np.degrees(phi):.1f}°")
print(f"Verify: x(0) = {x[0]:.3f} (should be {x0})")
print(f"Verify: v(0) = {v[0]:.3f} (should be {v0})")

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=x, line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2),
                          name='x(t)'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=v, line=dict(color='#ff00ff', width=2),
                          name='v(t)'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0], y=[x0], mode='markers',
                          marker=dict(size=12, color='#ffff00'),
                          name=f'x₀ = {x0}'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0], y=[v0], mode='markers',
                          marker=dict(size=12, color='#ff6b6b', symbol='diamond'),
                          name=f'v₀ = {v0}'))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    title=f'SHO: A={A:.2f}, φ={np.degrees(phi):.0f}°',
    xaxis=dict(title='Time (s)', gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Value', gridcolor='#2a2f4a',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080')
)
fig.show()
