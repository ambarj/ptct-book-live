Conservation Laws: Energy & Angular Momentum

Why does a planet's orbit lie in a single, unchanging plane for billions of years? Why does a comet whip past the Sun at perihelion and crawl at aphelion? Both questions have the same one-word answer: conservation. For any central force, the angular momentum vector \( \vec L \) and the total energy \( E \) are constants of motion. The first conservation law forces orbits to be planar; the second, combined with the first, controls how fast the body moves at every point along its path. Together they cut a six-dimensional phase-space problem down to one effective degree of freedom — and they give us a built-in correctness check for any numerical orbit we compute.

💡
Learning Objectives:
  • State and apply angular-momentum conservation: \( d\vec L/dt = \vec r \times \vec F = 0 \) when \( \vec F \parallel \hat r \)
  • Recognize that \( \vec L \) constant ⇒ orbit lies in a fixed plane perpendicular to \( \vec L \)
  • Reduce the planar problem to polar coordinates: \( \ell = \mu\rho^2\dot\phi \) and \( E = \tfrac{1}{2}\mu\dot\rho^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\mu\rho^2\dot\phi^2 + U(\rho) \)
  • Use conservation of E and ℓ as a numerical sanity check — drift in either is a bug, not physics
  • See Kepler's second law (equal areas in equal times) as a one-line consequence of angular-momentum conservation

Symmetry Implies Conservation

Behind every conservation law lurks a symmetry of nature. We will not prove this here — it's the content of Noether's theorem, one of the deepest results in classical and quantum physics — but the dictionary is short and worth remembering:

  • Translation symmetry of empty space ⇒ linear momentum is conserved.
  • Rotation symmetry of \( U(r) \) (depends on \( |\vec r| \), not direction) ⇒ angular momentum is conserved.
  • Time-translation symmetry (laws don't change from one moment to the next) ⇒ energy is conserved.

Central force motion enjoys all three. Linear momentum of the two-body system is conserved (CM moves uniformly — already used in Section 8.1). For the relative motion in the CM frame, both energy and angular momentum are conserved.

Why \( \vec L \) is constant for a central force:

$$ \vec L = \vec r \times \vec p, \qquad \frac{d\vec L}{dt} = \vec r \times \vec F $$

For a central force \( \vec F = f(r)\hat r \), the cross product \( \vec r \times \hat r = 0 \). So \( d\vec L/dt = 0 \) — angular momentum is conserved automatically, regardless of the specific form of \( U(r) \).

Toggle the angular-momentum vector \( \vec L \) and the orbital plane (perpendicular to \( \vec L \)) — no matter how you tilt the initial conditions, the orbit stays in that plane.

From 3D to 2D: Polar Coordinates in the Orbital Plane

Once we know the motion is planar, we choose the orbital plane as the \( xy \)-plane and switch from Cartesian \( (x, y) \) to polar \( (\rho, \phi) \). The kinetic energy in polar coordinates becomes \( v^2 = \dot\rho^2 + \rho^2\dot\phi^2 \) (a result that's worth deriving once with the chain rule). Combined with the potential, the conservation laws read:

Conservation of angular momentum:

$$ \boxed{\ell = \mu\rho^2\dot\phi = \text{const}} $$

Conservation of energy:

$$ \boxed{E = \tfrac{1}{2}\mu\dot\rho^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\mu\rho^2\dot\phi^2 + U(\rho) = \text{const}} $$

Two scalar conservation laws collapse a four-dimensional phase space \( (\rho, \dot\rho, \phi, \dot\phi) \) to one effective coordinate. Section 8.4 will use this to define the effective potential.

Explorer: Conservation as a Numerical Sanity Check

Integrate a planar central-force orbit two ways: with bare Euler (first-order) and with RK4 (fourth-order — the workhorse of Chapter 4). Both should give the same orbit if the integrator is exact. They don't, and the amount by which \( E \) and \( \ell \) drift tells you the magnitude of numerical error. Halve the step \( h \) — Euler error roughly halves; RK4 error drops by a factor of 16.

For a perfectly circular orbit in any central potential, both \( E \) and \( \ell \) are constant of course. Which of these is also constant?
Explanation: A circular orbit means \( \rho \) is constant in time (no radial motion), so \( \dot\rho = 0 \). Then from \( \ell = \mu\rho^2\dot\phi \), constancy of \( \rho \) and \( \ell \) forces \( \dot\phi \) constant — the planet sweeps angle at a uniform rate. Compare this to an elliptical orbit, where \( \rho \) varies, so \( \dot\phi = \ell/(\mu\rho^2) \) is faster at perihelion (small ρ) and slower at aphelion (large ρ) — Kepler's second law in action.
For Earth orbiting the Sun (μ ≈ M_⊕ = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, ρ ≈ 1.50 × 10¹¹ m, period \( T = 1 \) yr ≈ 3.15 × 10⁷ s): the angular speed for a (nearly) circular orbit is \( \dot\phi = 2\pi/T \approx \) rad/s; the magnitude of angular momentum is \( \ell = \mu\rho^2\dot\phi \approx \) J·s. (Use scientific notation like 2.0e-7.)
Explanation: \( \dot\phi = 2\pi/(3.15 \times 10^7) \approx 1.99 \times 10^{-7} \) rad/s. Then \( \ell = (5.97 \times 10^{24})(1.50 \times 10^{11})^2(1.99 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 2.67 \times 10^{40} \) J·s. This is a huge number, and yet \( \ell/\hbar \approx 2.5 \times 10^{74} \) — the Earth has a quantum number of order \( 10^{74} \) for its orbital angular momentum, which is why the classical limit is so accurate for planets.
Your numerical orbit shows \( |\Delta E/E| \) growing slowly with time. Which conclusions are valid? (Select all)
Explanation:
  • Numerical error: ✓ The physics is exactly conservative; any drift in \( E \) or \( \ell \) is the integrator falling short.
  • Physics violated: ✗ Newton's laws + central force ⇒ exact conservation. Code is approximate; physics is not.
  • Smaller h: ✓ Euler's global error is \( O(h) \); RK4's is \( O(h^4) \). Halving h cuts Euler error in half, RK4 error by 16×.
  • Better integrator: ✓ For long-duration orbits, even RK4 drifts. Symplectic integrators (e.g., Verlet, leapfrog) preserve energy up to bounded oscillations, never secular drift. They are the gold standard for celestial mechanics.
  • Unbounded orbit: ✗ Drift in \( E \) tells you about your code, not about whether the orbit is bound. Use \( E < 0 \) for the Kepler problem to determine boundedness, not the drift magnitude.

Kepler's Second Law: A Free Gift

Consider the area swept by the radius vector in time \( dt \). In polar coordinates this is the triangle with sides \( \rho \) and \( \rho\,d\phi \), so

$$ \frac{dA}{dt} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho^2\dot\phi = \frac{\ell}{2\mu} = \text{const} $$
💡
Equal Areas in Equal Times.

Kepler announced this in 1609 from Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars, three generations before Newton. It is one of his three laws of planetary motion. We just derived it in one line from angular-momentum conservation — and our derivation never used the specific form \( U \propto -1/\rho \). Kepler's second law holds for any central force: gravitational, Coulomb, harmonic spring, anything. Only the first and third laws are specific to the inverse-square attraction.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# RK4 on 3D Kepler problem; verify L is conserved and orbit is planar.
def force(r):
    return -r / (r @ r) ** 1.5

def rhs(s):
    r, v = s[:3], s[3:]
    return np.concatenate([v, force(r)])

def rk4_step(s, h):
    k1 = rhs(s)
    k2 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k1)
    k3 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k2)
    k4 = rhs(s + h*k3)
    return s + (h/6) * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

# Tilted initial conditions
s = np.array([1.0, 0, 0.3, 0, 0.8, 0.2])
h, N = 0.01, 4000
traj = np.zeros((N, 6))
for k in range(N):
    traj[k] = s; s = rk4_step(s, h)

# Angular momentum (constant)
L = np.cross(traj[:, :3], traj[:, 3:])
print("L initial:", L[0])
print("L final:  ", L[-1])
print("|L| relative drift:", np.abs(np.linalg.norm(L[-1]) - np.linalg.norm(L[0])) / np.linalg.norm(L[0]))

fig = go.Figure(go.Scatter3d(
    x=traj[:, 0], y=traj[:, 1], z=traj[:, 2],
    mode='lines', line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=3)))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter3d(
    x=[0, L[0, 0]], y=[0, L[0, 1]], z=[0, L[0, 2]],
    mode='lines+markers', line=dict(color='#ffbe0b', width=4), name='L⃗'))
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark', height=500,
    scene=dict(
        xaxis=dict(zerolinecolor='#808080', range=[-2, 2]),
        yaxis=dict(zerolinecolor='#808080', range=[-2, 2]),
        zaxis=dict(zerolinecolor='#808080', range=[-2, 2])))
fig.show()

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

# Compare Euler vs RK4 for energy/L conservation in planar Kepler.
def F(r):    # radial force from U = -1/r
    return -r / (r @ r) ** 1.5

def euler_step(s, h):
    r, v = s[:2], s[2:]
    return np.concatenate([r + h*v, v + h*F(r)])

def rk4_step(s, h):
    def rhs(s):
        return np.concatenate([s[2:], F(s[:2])])
    k1 = rhs(s); k2 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k1)
    k3 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k2); k4 = rhs(s + h*k3)
    return s + (h/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

def integrate(stepper, s0, h, N):
    out = np.zeros((N, 4)); s = s0.copy()
    for k in range(N):
        out[k] = s; s = stepper(s, h)
    return out

s0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.9])  # initial r, v
h, N = 0.05, 1000
eu = integrate(euler_step, s0, h, N)
rk = integrate(rk4_step, s0, h, N)

def E(s):  # energy per μ
    r, v = s[:, :2], s[:, 2:]
    return 0.5*(v*v).sum(1) - 1/np.sqrt((r*r).sum(1))

def L(s):  # z-angular momentum per μ
    return s[:, 0]*s[:, 3] - s[:, 1]*s[:, 2]

t = np.arange(N) * h
print(f"Euler  ΔE/E max: {np.max(np.abs((E(eu) - E(eu)[0])/E(eu)[0])):.2e}")
print(f"RK4    ΔE/E max: {np.max(np.abs((E(rk) - E(rk)[0])/E(rk)[0])):.2e}")

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Recompute the RK4 Kepler trajectory (same as the previous tab) so this runs standalone
def F(r):
    return -r / (r @ r) ** 1.5

def rk4_step(s, h):
    def rhs(s):
        return np.concatenate([s[2:], F(s[:2])])
    k1 = rhs(s); k2 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k1)
    k3 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k2); k4 = rhs(s + h*k3)
    return s + (h/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

s0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.9])
h, N = 0.05, 1000
rk = np.zeros((N, 4)); s = s0.copy()
for kk in range(N):
    rk[kk] = s; s = rk4_step(s, h)

# Verify Kepler's 2nd law: dA/dt = ℓ/(2μ) is constant.
# Sweep an elliptical orbit; compute area swept per unit time numerically.

# Use the trajectory `rk` from the previous tab (planar).
# Area between adjacent radii: (1/2) |r_n × r_{n+1}|
r = rk[:, :2]
dA = 0.5 * np.abs(r[:-1, 0] * r[1:, 1] - r[:-1, 1] * r[1:, 0])
dA_dt = dA / h

t = np.arange(len(dA)) * h
fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=dA_dt, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2), name='dA/dt (measured)'))
fig.add_hline(y=dA_dt.mean(), line=dict(color='#ff006e', dash='dash'),
              annotation_text=f'mean = {dA_dt.mean():.4f}')
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='t', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='dA/dt', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    title="Kepler's 2nd law: equal areas in equal times",
    height=400)
fig.show()
print(f"std/mean of dA/dt: {dA_dt.std()/dA_dt.mean():.2e}  (should be ≈ 0)")
