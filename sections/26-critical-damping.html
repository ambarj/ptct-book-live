Critical Damping in Engineering

You've seen three damping regimes. Now the engineering question: which one should you choose? The answer, almost universally, is critical damping — or something very close to it.

A door closer that oscillates (underdamped) lets the door slam back and forth. One that's overdamped takes forever to close, leaving you standing in a draft. Critical damping closes the door as fast as possible without any bounce. The same logic applies to car suspensions, galvanometers, earthquake dampers, and RLC circuits.

Learning Objectives
  • Use the damping ratio \( \zeta = \gamma / \omega_0 \) to characterize a system's behavior
  • Design a door closer that achieves critical damping by choosing spring stiffness and damping coefficient
  • Tune a car suspension for a target overshoot percentage
  • Meet engineering specifications (settling time, maximum overshoot) by adjusting \( \zeta \)

The damping ratio:

$$ \zeta = \frac{\gamma}{\omega_0} = \frac{b}{2\sqrt{km}} $$

Three regimes revisited:
\( \zeta < 1 \): underdamped (oscillates) — overshoot = \( 100 \, e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \)%
\( \zeta = 1 \): critically damped (fastest non-oscillatory return)
\( \zeta > 1 \): overdamped (sluggish, no overshoot)

Designing a Door Closer

A door with mass \( m = 5 \) kg swings on hinges with a return spring (stiffness \( k \)) and a hydraulic damper (damping coefficient \( b \)). Your job: choose \( k \) and \( b \) so the door closes smoothly. Too little damping and it bounces. Too much and it creeps. The sweet spot is \( b = b_{\text{crit}} = 2\sqrt{km} \).

The left panel shows how the door angle decays over time. The dashed green curve is the ideal (critical damping for that \( k \)). The right panel shows performance metrics: overshoot percentage, settling time, and the damping ratio \( \zeta \).

Explorer 1: Door Closer Designer
Key Insight

For critical damping, \( b_{\text{crit}} = 2\sqrt{km} \). Notice that a stiffer spring (larger \( k \)) requires a stronger damper. This makes sense: a stiffer spring pushes harder, so the damper needs more force to prevent overshoot. Set \( k = 20 \) N/m and you'll find \( b_{\text{crit}} = 2\sqrt{20 \times 5} = 20 \) Ns/m — a satisfying coincidence.

Tuning a Car Suspension

When a car hits a bump, each wheel's suspension compresses and then recovers. The mass \( m \) is the car's weight on that wheel, the spring \( k \) supports the weight, and the shock absorber provides damping \( b \). Most cars target \( \zeta \approx 0.3 \) (sporty, some bounce) to \( \zeta \approx 0.7 \) (comfortable, minimal bounce). Exactly \( \zeta = 1 \) would feel "dead" — no road feedback at all.

The left panel shows suspension displacement after a 10 cm bump, with reference curves at three standard damping ratios. The right panel shows overshoot percentage as a function of \( \zeta \).

Explorer 2: Suspension Tuner
Key Insight

The overshoot formula \( \text{OS}(\%) = 100 \, e^{-\pi\zeta / \sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \) is one of the most useful results in control engineering. At \( \zeta = 0.3 \), overshoot is about 37% — noticeably bouncy. At \( \zeta = 0.7 \), it drops to about 5% — barely perceptible. At \( \zeta = 1 \), overshoot is zero. Real car suspensions sit around \( \zeta = 0.2 \)–\( 0.4 \) because some oscillation gives the driver road "feel."

The Design Challenge

Real engineering means meeting specifications. Below, you'll be given random design specs: a system with a certain \( \omega_0 \), a maximum settling time, and a maximum overshoot. Your job is to choose the damping ratio \( \zeta \) that satisfies both constraints. Too little damping and you violate the overshoot spec. Too much and you blow the settling time.

Explorer 3: Design Challenge
Key Insight

In practice, engineers rarely aim for exactly \( \zeta = 1 \). Many systems work best at \( \zeta \approx 0.7 \): small overshoot (about 5%), fast settling, and good disturbance rejection. This is the "standard second-order design" used throughout control engineering — from autopilots to servo motors to audio amplifiers.

If the spec says "overshoot ≤ 0%", what is the minimum \( \zeta \) you can use?
Explanation
Overshoot is zero only when \( \zeta \geq 1 \). Any \( \zeta < 1 \) produces nonzero overshoot (the formula \( 100 e^{-\pi\zeta / \sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \) is always positive for \( \zeta < 1 \)). So the minimum \( \zeta \) for zero overshoot is exactly 1.0 — critical damping.
For a given \( \omega_0 \), if you must settle within 2 seconds and have no overshoot, which is the best strategy?
Explanation
Critical damping (\( \zeta = 1 \)) is the fastest non-oscillatory response. Higher \( \zeta \) is slower (overdamped = sluggish), and lower \( \zeta \) overshoots. So \( \zeta = 1 \) is the optimal choice when you need both speed and no overshoot. Whether it meets the 2-second deadline depends on \( \omega_0 \).

Code It Yourself

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

def damped(t, x0, gamma, omega0):
    disc = gamma**2 - omega0**2
    if disc < 0:
        wd = np.sqrt(-disc)
        return x0 * np.exp(-gamma*t) * (np.cos(wd*t) + gamma/wd * np.sin(wd*t))
    elif disc > 0:
        s = np.sqrt(disc)
        r1, r2 = -gamma + s, -gamma - s
        return x0/(r2-r1) * (r2*np.exp(r1*t) - r1*np.exp(r2*t))
    else:
        return x0 * (1 + gamma*t) * np.exp(-gamma*t)

m, k = 5.0, 20.0
omega0 = np.sqrt(k/m)
b_crit = 2 * np.sqrt(k * m)

t = np.linspace(0, 8, 500)
fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2,
    subplot_titles=['Door Angle vs Time', 'Performance'])

for b, name, color in [
    (b_crit*0.5, 'Underdamped', '#00f3ff'),
    (b_crit, 'Critical', '#00ff9f'),
    (b_crit*2, 'Overdamped', '#ff00ff')]:
    gamma = b / (2*m)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=damped(t, 1, gamma, omega0),
        name=f'{name} (b={b:.0f})', line=dict(color=color)),
        row=1, col=1)

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title=f'Door Closer Design (m={m} kg, k={k} N/m, b_crit={b_crit:.0f} Ns/m)')
fig.update_xaxes(title='Time (s)', gridcolor='#2a2f4a', linecolor='#808080')
fig.update_yaxes(title='Angle (norm.)', gridcolor='#2a2f4a', linecolor='#808080')
fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

def overshoot_pct(zeta):
    if zeta >= 1: return 0
    return 100 * np.exp(-np.pi * zeta / np.sqrt(1 - zeta**2))

# Overshoot vs damping ratio
zetas = np.linspace(0.01, 2.0, 200)
os = [overshoot_pct(z) for z in zetas]

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=zetas, y=os,
    line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', width=2.5), name='Overshoot %'))
fig.add_vline(x=1.0, line=dict(color='#00ff9f', dash='dash', width=1.5),
    annotation_text='Critical (ζ=1)')

# Mark common design points
for z, label in [(0.3, 'Sporty'), (0.7, 'Standard'), (1.0, 'Critical')]:
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[z], y=[overshoot_pct(z)],
        mode='markers+text', marker=dict(size=10),
        text=[f'{label}: {overshoot_pct(z):.1f}%'],
        textposition='top right', showlegend=False))

fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    title='Overshoot vs Damping Ratio',
    xaxis=dict(title='ζ', range=[0, 2],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Overshoot %', range=[0, 55],
        gridcolor='#2a2f4a', linecolor='#808080'))
fig.show()
