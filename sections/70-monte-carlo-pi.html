Estimating π: Geometric Monte Carlo

In Section 6.1 you threw darts to estimate π. Now let’s go deeper. What happens when you repeat the experiment hundreds of times? The distribution of estimates reveals the central limit theorem in action. And by extending from circles to hyperspheres, you’ll see Monte Carlo tackle a problem where analytical formulas exist but become impractical — computing volumes in high dimensions.

Learning Objectives
  • Run large-scale (> 100,000 samples) Monte Carlo π estimation and quantify the final accuracy
  • Repeat the experiment many times and observe the distribution of estimates is Gaussian (CLT)
  • Extend the dart-throwing idea to hypersphere volumes in d dimensions
  • Discover that hypersphere volume shrinks dramatically with dimension — a counterintuitive geometric fact

Hypersphere Volume via MC:

$$ V_d = 2^d \times \frac{N_{\text{inside}}}{N_{\text{total}}} $$

Throw \( N \) random points uniformly in the \( d \)-dimensional cube \( [-1, 1]^d \). Count how many satisfy \( x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_d^2 \leq 1 \). The fraction times the cube volume \( 2^d \) estimates the hypersphere volume.

Run with 1,000 darts, then 100,000. How many correct digits of π do you typically get with 100,000?
Explanation: With N = 100,000: error ≈ \( 1/\sqrt{100000} \approx 0.003 \). So the estimate is 3.14 ± 0.01 — about 2–3 correct digits. Getting 6 digits would need \( N \sim 10^{12} \) — a trillion darts! MC is not the way to compute π to high precision.
With N = 100,000, the expected error is \( 1/\sqrt{N} \approx \) × 10&sup-3;. (Compute √100,000 ≈ 316)
Explanation: \( \sqrt{100{,}000} = \sqrt{10^5} = 10^{2.5} \approx 316 \). So \( 1/\sqrt{N} \approx 1/316 \approx 0.00316 \approx 316 \times 10^{-5} \). Multiplying by σ ≈ 1.6 gives error ≈ 0.005, consistent with about 2–3 digits of accuracy.
The accuracy plot shows error vs N for multiple runs. What shape does the envelope follow?
Explanation: The error envelope follows \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) — the universal MC convergence rate from Section 6.3. On the log-log plot, this is a straight line with slope −1/2.
Explorer: Distribution of Estimates (CLT in Action)

Repeat the π experiment many times. Each trial gives a slightly different estimate. The histogram of estimates is Gaussian — a direct consequence of the central limit theorem. The width of the Gaussian is \( \sigma / \sqrt{N} \).

Run 500 trials with N = 100 per trial, then 500 trials with N = 10,000. How does the histogram change?
Explanation: Width \( \propto 1/\sqrt{N} \). Going from N = 100 to N = 10,000 is a 100× increase, so the width shrinks by \( \sqrt{100} = 10 \). The center remains at π (unbiased). Both histograms are Gaussian — just different widths.
The shape of the histogram is , as guaranteed by the central limit theorem.
Explanation: The CLT says that the mean of N independent samples has a Gaussian (normal) distribution, regardless of the original distribution. Each MC estimate is a sample mean, so the distribution of estimates is Gaussian with mean = true value and std = σ/√N.
Which statements about the distribution of MC estimates are true? (Select all)
Explanation:
  • Mean = true value: ✓ MC is unbiased — the expected value of each estimate is exact.
  • Always within 1%: ✗ Individual runs can be off by more. About 5% of runs exceed 2σ.
  • 68% within 1σ: ✓ This is the Gaussian 68-95-99.7 rule. One standard error captures ~68%.
  • Uniform for large N: ✗ It becomes a narrower Gaussian, never uniform.
Explorer: Volume of a Hypersphere

The volume of a unit sphere in \( d \) dimensions has the surprising formula \( V_d = \pi^{d/2} / \Gamma(d/2 + 1) \). In 2D: \( \pi \). In 3D: \( 4\pi/3 \). But above \( d \approx 5 \), the volume shrinks toward zero! Use MC to estimate these volumes and witness this counterintuitive fact.

Test Your Understanding

Use the Hypersphere explorer to answer these questions.

Run with 50,000 samples. What happens to the fraction of points inside the unit sphere as dimension increases from 2 to 10?
Explanation: In high dimensions, almost all volume of the cube is in the corners, far from the origin. The sphere occupies a vanishingly small fraction: in 10D, only about 0.25% of random points land inside the unit sphere. This is the curse of dimensionality seen geometrically — spheres become “thin” compared to cubes.
The hypersphere volume peaks at dimension d ≈ and then decreases toward zero. (Read from the bar chart)
Explanation: The exact peak is at \( d \approx 5.26 \) (between d = 5 and d = 6). The volume \( V_d = \pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2+1) \) increases from d = 1 to about d = 5, then decreases. By d = 20, the volume is astronomically small. This is one of the most counterintuitive facts in high-dimensional geometry.
In 10D, the MC hit rate is less than 1%. Why is this a problem for the hit-or-miss method?
Explanation: With a ~0.25% hit rate, you need enormous N to get enough hits for a reliable estimate. This is the high-dimensional version of the hit-or-miss inefficiency from Section 6.2. Importance sampling (Section 6.4) or specialized techniques can help by concentrating samples near the sphere.

Python Code

Python 

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Distribution of π estimates over 500 trials
n_trials = 500
Ns = [100, 1000, 10000]

fig = go.Figure()
colors = ['#ff006e', '#ffbe0b', '#00f3ff']
for N, color in zip(Ns, colors):
    estimates = []
    for _ in range(n_trials):
        x = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, N)
        y = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, N)
        pi_est = 4 * np.mean(x**2 + y**2 <= 1)
        estimates.append(pi_est)
    fig.add_trace(go.Histogram(
        x=estimates, opacity=0.5, nbinsx=30,
        marker_color=color, name=f'N = {N:,}'))

fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=[np.pi, np.pi], y=[0, n_trials * 0.3],
    mode='lines', line=dict(color='white', width=2, dash='dash'),
    name='True π'))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='Estimate of π', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Count', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    barmode='overlay', height=450, width=600)
fig.show()
Python 

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from math import gamma, pi

# MC estimation of hypersphere volumes
N = 100000
dims = range(1, 16)
mc_volumes, true_volumes = [], []

for d in dims:
    # Random points in [-1, 1]^d
    pts = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, (N, d))
    r2 = np.sum(pts**2, axis=1)
    frac_inside = np.mean(r2 <= 1)
    mc_vol = (2**d) * frac_inside
    mc_volumes.append(mc_vol)

    # Exact volume
    true_vol = pi**(d/2) / gamma(d/2 + 1)
    true_volumes.append(true_vol)
    print(f'd={d:2d}: MC = {mc_vol:.4f}, True = {true_vol:.4f}, '
          f'Hit rate = {frac_inside*100:.2f}%')

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Bar(
    x=list(dims), y=mc_volumes,
    marker_color='#00f3ff', name='MC estimate'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=list(dims), y=true_volumes,
    mode='markers+lines',
    marker=dict(color='white', size=8),
    line=dict(color='white', width=2, dash='dash'),
    name='Exact'))

fig.update_layout(
    template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='Dimension d', dtick=1,
               zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Volume of unit hypersphere',
               zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    height=450, width=600)
fig.show()
