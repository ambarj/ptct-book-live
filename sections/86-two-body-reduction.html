The Two-Body Problem: Reduction to One Body

The Earth orbits the Sun. The Moon orbits the Earth. An electron orbits a proton. Two stars orbit each other. Every two-body gravitational or electrostatic system looks coupled at first glance — a pair of differential equations whose right-hand sides depend on both bodies' positions. In this section we'll see that a clever change of coordinates cuts the problem in half: the center of mass drifts uniformly through space, while the relative motion reduces to a single particle of reduced mass μ moving in a central potential. This is the most important coordinate change in classical mechanics.

💡
Learning Objectives:
  • Set up the coupled equations of motion for two gravitating bodies
  • Define the center-of-mass and relative coordinates and verify the system decouples
  • Compute the reduced mass \( \mu = m_1 m_2 / (m_1 + m_2) \) and understand its limiting cases
  • Recognize that everything from this point on uses the equivalent one-body problem of mass μ in a potential U(r)

The Hook: Earth and Moon

Let the Earth have mass \( m_E \) and position \( \vec r_E \), and the Moon have mass \( m_M \) and position \( \vec r_M \), measured in some Sun-centered inertial frame. Ignoring the Sun's gravity (a simplification that lets us focus on the Earth–Moon dance), Newton's second law plus Newton's law of gravitation give:

Coupled equations of motion:

$$ m_E\,\ddot{\vec r}_E = -\frac{G\,m_E\,m_M}{|\vec r_E - \vec r_M|^3}(\vec r_E - \vec r_M) $$ $$ m_M\,\ddot{\vec r}_M = +\frac{G\,m_E\,m_M}{|\vec r_E - \vec r_M|^3}(\vec r_E - \vec r_M) $$

These are six coupled second-order ODEs (three components per body). The right-hand sides depend on both bodies' positions. Can you simplify?

  • One-body in disguise: ✓ This is the punchline of the whole reduction — the entire rest of Chapter 8 studies this single equation.

The Reduction in One Page

Define new variables. Instead of two position vectors \( \vec r_1, \vec r_2 \), use the center of mass and the relative coordinate:

$$ \vec R = \frac{m_1 \vec r_1 + m_2 \vec r_2}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad \vec r = \vec r_1 - \vec r_2 $$

Add the two coupled EOMs (multiplied by m₁ and m₂) — the internal forces cancel by Newton's third law, leaving \( M\ddot{\vec R} = 0 \). Subtract them — the masses recombine into a single coefficient. The result is a remarkable separation:

Decoupled equations of motion:

$$ \boxed{\ddot{\vec R} = 0, \qquad \mu\,\ddot{\vec r} = \vec f(r)} $$

where the reduced mass is

$$ \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \qquad \Leftrightarrow \qquad \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2} $$
💡
Key Insight: 6 ODEs → 3 ODEs.

What looked like six coupled second-order ODEs splits into two independent pieces: three trivial ones for the CM (uniform motion, immediately solved), and three for \( \vec r(t) \) — the same form as a single particle of mass μ in the potential \( U(r) \). Newton used this in the Principia (1687) to handle the Earth–Moon system. Every chapter from here on studies the equivalent one-body equation.

For the Sun (M_☉ = 1.99×10³⁰ kg) and the Earth (M_⊕ = 5.97×10²⁴ kg): the reduced mass μ is essentially equal to M_⊕, differing by only about parts per million. (Hint: μ/m_2 = m_1/(m_1+m_2) ≈ 1 − m_2/m_1.)
Explanation: \( m_2/m_1 = 5.97 \times 10^{24}/1.99 \times 10^{30} = 3.0 \times 10^{-6} \) ≈ 3 ppm. So \( \mu = m_2 \cdot (1 - m_2/m_1) \approx m_2 - 3 \) ppm. For practical purposes, in any planet-Sun system, \( \mu \approx \text{(planet mass)} \) and we often just call it "the planet's mass." But the correction matters for high-precision ephemerides and for spectroscopy of light atoms.
In the hydrogen atom (proton mass 1836× electron mass), the reduced mass is \( \mu = m_e \cdot 1836/1837 \approx 0.99946\, m_e \). What measurable consequence does this have?
Explanation: The Rydberg formula contains \( \mu \), not \( m_e \). The hydrogen Rydberg \( R_H = R_\infty \cdot \mu/m_e = R_\infty \cdot 1836/1837 \) is shifted by \( \sim 1/1837 \approx 0.054\% \) from the textbook \( R_\infty \) — observable in any decent spectrometer. Even more dramatically, deuterium (proton replaced by a deuteron, twice as heavy) has a slightly different shift, and the small wavelength difference between H and D Lyman lines was how Urey discovered deuterium in 1931. Reduced mass is a real, measurable quantity.
Now think about three bodies (e.g., Sun-Earth-Moon, or three stars). Which statements are true? (Select all)
Explanation:
  • CM still uniform: ✓ Newton's third law works for any number of bodies — internal forces cancel pairwise, so total momentum is conserved.
  • Reduces to two one-bodies: ✗ This is the deep failure: there's no clever change of coordinates that decouples three bodies. The reason: three bodies have 9 position components, but only 6 conserved quantities (CM position, CM momentum, energy, angular momentum) — not enough to make the system integrable.
  • No closed form: ✓ Poincaré won the 1887 King Oscar Prize by proving the general 3-body problem is non-integrable. Solutions exist numerically and in special cases (Lagrange points, figure-8 orbit), but no general formula.
  • Always chaotic: ✗ Some configurations are stable (e.g., Sun-Earth-Moon over millions of years; the Lagrange L4/L5 points). Chaos depends on the configuration.

What's Next: Non-Dimensionalization

💡
Forward Reference: μ disappears too.

In Chapter 1 (Section 1.6) we learned that smart unit choices can absorb dimensional parameters into the variables themselves. The same trick works here. Starting in Section 8.3, we will rescale the radial coordinate, time, and energy by combinations of \( \mu \), \( \ell \) (angular momentum), and \( E \) (energy) — and the equations become parameter-free except for the shape of the central potential \( U(r) \). The rescaling is:

$$ \rho \to \frac{\ell}{\sqrt{\mu E}}\,\rho, \qquad t \to \frac{\ell}{E}\,t, \qquad U \to E\,U $$

For now, just remember the main lesson of this section: from this point onward, μ is the only mass that appears. Whether you're studying the Sun-Earth system or the hydrogen atom or a binary pulsar, the math is identical — only μ and the form of \( U(r) \) change.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Solve coupled two-body system in 2D using RK4
# State: [x1, y1, vx1, vy1, x2, y2, vx2, vy2]

G  = 1.0        # use G = 1 units
m1 = 1.0
m2 = 0.3        # try varying this

def rhs(state):
    x1, y1, vx1, vy1, x2, y2, vx2, vy2 = state
    dx, dy = x2 - x1, y2 - y1
    r3 = (dx*dx + dy*dy) ** 1.5
    fx, fy = G * dx / r3, G * dy / r3
    return np.array([vx1, vy1,  m2*fx,  m2*fy,
                     vx2, vy2, -m1*fx, -m1*fy])

def rk4_step(s, h):
    k1 = rhs(s)
    k2 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k1)
    k3 = rhs(s + 0.5*h*k2)
    k4 = rhs(s + h*k3)
    return s + (h/6) * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

# Initial conditions: bodies on x-axis, perpendicular velocities → orbit
s = np.array([-m2/(m1+m2), 0, 0, -0.7,
              +m1/(m1+m2), 0, 0, +0.7*m1/m2])
h, N = 0.02, 1500
traj = np.zeros((N, 8))
for k in range(N):
    traj[k] = s; s = rk4_step(s, h)

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=traj[:,0], y=traj[:,1], mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2), name='m₁'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=traj[:,4], y=traj[:,5], mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#ff006e', width=2), name='m₂'))
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(range=[-2, 2], scaleanchor='y',
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(range=[-2, 2],
               zerolinecolor='#808080', linecolor='#808080'),
    height=500)
fig.show()

def reduced_mass(m1, m2):
    """μ = m1·m2 / (m1 + m2). Always smaller than min(m1, m2)."""
    return m1 * m2 / (m1 + m2)

# Canonical systems (masses in kg)
M_SUN = 1.989e30
M_EARTH = 5.972e24
M_MOON = 7.342e22
M_PLUTO = 1.303e22
M_CHARON = 1.586e21
M_PROTON = 1.673e-27
M_ELECTRON = 9.109e-31

systems = [
    ("Sun-Earth",    M_SUN,    M_EARTH),
    ("Earth-Moon",   M_EARTH,  M_MOON),
    ("Pluto-Charon", M_PLUTO,  M_CHARON),
    ("Equal binary", M_SUN,    M_SUN),
    ("Hydrogen atom", M_PROTON, M_ELECTRON),
]

print(f"{'system':18s}  {'m1':>11s}  {'m2':>11s}  {'μ':>11s}  {'μ/m_lighter':>12s}")
for name, m1, m2 in systems:
    mu = reduced_mass(m1, m2)
    ratio = mu / min(m1, m2)
    print(f"{name:18s}  {m1:>11.3e}  {m2:>11.3e}  {mu:>11.3e}  {ratio:>12.6f}")
