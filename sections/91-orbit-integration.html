Numerical Integration of Orbits

Section 8.5 told us what Kepler orbits should look like — closed ellipses for \( E < 0 \), parabolas at \( E = 0 \), hyperbolas for \( E > 0 \). Now we'll build a numerical orbit-launcher and watch the same physics emerge from RK4 (the workhorse from Chapter 4) applied to Newton's equations directly. The Section 8.5 analytical formulas are our ground truth; the conservation laws from Section 8.2 (constant \( E \) and \( \ell \)) are our built-in sanity check. By the end you'll have a tool that integrates any central-force orbit — and the confidence that the numerics agree with theory wherever theory exists.

  • Recognize when RK4 in Cartesian struggles (high eccentricity, very long integrations) and what alternatives exist (symplectic integrators)

Why Cartesian Instead of \( (\rho, \phi) \)?

A natural first instinct is to integrate the dimensionless Kepler problem in the state vector \( (t, \phi, \rho) \) directly — using the polar machinery from Section 8.3 with right-hand side

$$ \frac{dt}{dt} = 1, \qquad \frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{1}{\rho^2}, \qquad \frac{d\rho}{dt} = \sqrt{2\!\left(E - \frac{1}{2\rho^2} + \frac{1}{\rho}\right)} $$

This looks elegant — it exploits the angular-momentum reduction explicitly. But it has a hidden pitfall: the square root in \( d\rho/dt \) goes imaginary at turning points (where \( E = U_{\text{eff}}(\rho) \)) because the radicand passes through zero with the wrong sign on the way down. A naive integrator silently fails there. There's a fix (track the sign of \( \dot\rho \) manually and flip when it changes), but it adds complication.

The robust alternative is Cartesian: integrate \( (x, y, v_x, v_y) \) as a 4-D ODE system using Newton's law of gravity directly, with no sign ambiguity:

Cartesian equations of motion (Kepler, μ = k = 1):

$$ \ddot x = -\frac{x}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}}, \qquad \ddot y = -\frac{y}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}} $$

State-vector form for RK4:

$$ \vec s = (x, y, v_x, v_y), \qquad \frac{d\vec s}{dt} = (v_x,\;v_y,\;\ddot x,\;\ddot y) $$

Initial conditions are also more intuitive in Cartesian: just specify a starting position and a starting velocity. No need to compute angular momentum first.

Setting Up Initial Conditions

The cleanest setup launches the planet from perihelion (closest approach to the central body), with velocity perpendicular to the radius vector:

$$ \vec r(0) = (r_0, 0), \qquad \vec v(0) = (0, v_0) $$

Then the constants of motion at \( t = 0 \) are

$$ E = \tfrac{1}{2}v_0^2 - \frac{k}{r_0}, \qquad \ell = r_0\,v_0 $$

and the predicted orbit (from Section 8.5) has

$$ p = \frac{\ell^2}{\mu k}, \qquad e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2 E\,\ell^2}{\mu k^2}} $$
⚠️
Pitfall: choosing the right launch velocity.

For a circular orbit at radius \( r_0 \), set \( v_0 = \sqrt{k/r_0} \) (the circular-orbit velocity). Smaller \( v_0 \) ⇒ tiny ellipse (perihelion at \( r_0 \), aphelion smaller). Larger ⇒ ellipse extending outward. At \( v_0 = \sqrt{2k/r_0} \) the energy \( E = 0 \) — escape velocity, parabolic orbit. Above that, hyperbolic flyby. With \( k = 1, r_0 = 1 \): \( v_{\text{circ}} = 1 \), \( v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414 \).

Validating Against the Closed-Form Conic

Numerical integration is only as good as the validation behind it. We have a beautiful test: the same initial conditions \( (r_0, v_{x0}, v_{y0}) \) determine \( (E, \ell) \), which determine the analytical conic \( \rho(\phi) = p/(1+e\cos\phi) \). Plot the analytical curve on top of the numerical orbit — they should agree to many decimals over a single orbit.

From initial conditions to predicted conic:

$$ E = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_{x0}^2 + v_{y0}^2) - \frac{k}{r_0}, \qquad \ell = r_0 v_{y0} - 0\cdot v_{x0} = r_0 v_{y0} $$ $$ p = \frac{\ell^2}{\mu k}, \qquad e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2 E \ell^2}{\mu k^2}} $$

Predicted semi-major axis (for bound orbits):

$$ a = \frac{k}{2|E|} \quad \text{(for } E < 0\text{)} $$

Section 8.5 gave us the formulas; this section uses them as a yardstick. Below, the explorer plots both curves and reports the maximum radial discrepancy as a percentage of the orbit's semi-major axis.

When does fixed-step RK4 in Cartesian struggle? (Select all.)
Explanation:
  • High eccentricity: ✓ At perihelion, \( v \) can be enormous, and \( h \) needs to be small enough to resolve it — but a small \( h \) is wasteful at aphelion where motion is slow. Adaptive step-size methods solve this.
  • Collisions: ✓ Force \( \propto 1/r^3 \) → ∞ as \( r \to 0 \). Numerical singularities. Fixed-step RK4 will crash; need regularization (Levi-Civita or Kustaanheimo-Stiefel transforms).
  • Long-term drift: ✓ RK4's energy drifts at rate \( O(h^4) \) per orbit. Over millions of orbits, drift can accumulate to noticeable levels. Symplectic integrators have bounded oscillating error instead of secular drift.
  • Circular orbits: ✗ Circular orbits are the easiest case. RK4 handles them with negligible drift.
  • Smaller h is always better: ✗ Truncation error decreases as \( h \) shrinks, but roundoff error grows because you need more steps. Eventually, going smaller hurts. There's an optimal \( h \) (typically near \( 10^{-3} \) for double-precision physics integrations).

Beyond Kepler: A Universal Tool

💡
Forward Reference: the integrator generalizes.

Replace the Kepler force \( -k\vec r/r^3 \) with any central force, and the same RK4 code integrates the orbit. Section 8.7 will use this to explore non-Kepler potentials — Yukawa (\( -e^{-\rho/\rho_0}/\rho \), gives precessing rosettes), harmonic oscillator (\( +k\rho \), gives ellipses centered on the origin not at a focus). The numerical integrator is the universal tool; analytical solutions like Section 8.5's conic family are the rare and beautiful exception. For most central potentials in real physics — screened Coulomb in plasmas, intermolecular Lennard-Jones, even relativistic corrections — numerics is the only path forward.

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

# Cartesian RK4 for two-body Kepler with k = μ = 1
def F(s):  # s = [x, y, vx, vy]
    r3 = (s[0]*s[0] + s[1]*s[1]) ** 1.5
    return np.array([s[2], s[3], -s[0]/r3, -s[1]/r3])

def rk4_step(s, h):
    k1 = F(s)
    k2 = F(s + 0.5*h*k1)
    k3 = F(s + 0.5*h*k2)
    k4 = F(s + h*k3)
    return s + (h/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

# Initial conditions: launch from perihelion
r0, vy0 = 1.0, 1.2
s = np.array([r0, 0.0, 0.0, vy0])
h, N = 0.02, 1500
traj = np.zeros((N, 4))
for i in range(N):
    traj[i] = s; s = rk4_step(s, h)

x, y, vx, vy = traj[:,0], traj[:,1], traj[:,2], traj[:,3]
r = np.sqrt(x*x + y*y)
E = 0.5*(vx*vx + vy*vy) - 1/r
L = x*vy - y*vx

print(f"E_initial = {E[0]:.6f}, max drift |ΔE/E| = {np.max(np.abs((E - E[0])/E[0])):.2e}")
print(f"L_initial = {L[0]:.6f}, max drift |ΔL/L| = {np.max(np.abs((L - L[0])/L[0])):.2e}")

fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2, subplot_titles=['Orbit', 'Conservation'])
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=y, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2), name='orbit'), row=1, col=1)
t = np.arange(N) * h
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=(E-E[0])/E[0], mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#ff006e', width=1.5), name='ΔE/E'), row=1, col=2)
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=(L-L[0])/L[0], mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#ffbe0b', width=1.5), name='ΔL/L'), row=1, col=2)
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark', height=400)
fig.show()

import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Convergence study: max |ΔE/E| over one orbit vs step size h, RK4 vs Euler.
def F(s):
    r3 = (s[0]*s[0] + s[1]*s[1]) ** 1.5
    return np.array([s[2], s[3], -s[0]/r3, -s[1]/r3])

def rk4_step(s, h):
    k1 = F(s); k2 = F(s + 0.5*h*k1); k3 = F(s + 0.5*h*k2); k4 = F(s + h*k3)
    return s + (h/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

def euler_step(s, h):
    return s + h * F(s)

def integrate(stepper, s0, h, T):
    N = int(T / h)
    s = s0.copy(); E0 = 0.5*(s[2]**2 + s[3]**2) - 1/np.sqrt(s[0]**2 + s[1]**2)
    max_err = 0
    for _ in range(N):
        s = stepper(s, h)
        E = 0.5*(s[2]**2 + s[3]**2) - 1/np.sqrt(s[0]**2 + s[1]**2)
        max_err = max(max_err, abs((E - E0)/E0))
    return max_err

s0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0])  # circular orbit, T = 2π
hs = np.logspace(-3, -0.5, 12)
err_rk4   = [integrate(rk4_step,   s0, h, 2*np.pi) for h in hs]
err_euler = [integrate(euler_step, s0, h, 2*np.pi) for h in hs]

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=hs, y=err_rk4, mode='markers+lines',
    marker=dict(color='#00f3ff', size=8), name='RK4 (slope ≈ 4)'))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=hs, y=err_euler, mode='markers+lines',
    marker=dict(color='#ff006e', size=8), name='Euler (slope ≈ 1)'))
# Reference lines
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=hs, y=err_rk4[0]*(hs/hs[0])**4, mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', dash='dash', width=1), showlegend=False))
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=hs, y=err_euler[0]*(hs/hs[0]), mode='lines',
    line=dict(color='#ff006e', dash='dash', width=1), showlegend=False))
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='h', type='log', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    yaxis=dict(title='max |ΔE/E|', type='log', zerolinecolor='#808080'),
    title='RK4 (h⁴) vs Euler (h¹) convergence',
    height=420)
fig.show()
