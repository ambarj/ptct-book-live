Same Frequency Interference

In the last section you saw that two cosines at the same frequency add to give another cosine at that frequency. Now let's dig into the details: how does the phase difference between two waves control the resulting amplitude? This is the physics of interference — the mechanism behind noise-cancelling headphones, thin-film colours, and the double-slit experiment.

Learning Objectives
  • Derive the resultant amplitude formula for two same-frequency waves
  • Identify constructive and destructive interference from the phase difference
  • Represent wave addition as phasor (rotating vector) addition
  • Predict the phase difference needed to achieve a target amplitude

The amplitude formula:

$$ A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1 A_2 \cos(\Delta\phi) $$

where \( \Delta\phi = \phi_2 - \phi_1 \) is the phase difference. This single equation captures all of interference: when \( \cos(\Delta\phi) = +1 \) (constructive), \( A = A_1 + A_2 \); when \( \cos(\Delta\phi) = -1 \) (destructive), \( A = |A_1 - A_2| \).

Visualising Interference

Sweep the phase difference \( \Delta\phi \) from 0° to 360° and watch the sum wave grow and shrink. With equal amplitudes, the sum oscillates between double height (constructive) and complete cancellation (destructive). The dashed envelope shows the resultant amplitude.

Key Insight

The formula \( A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1 A_2 \cos(\Delta\phi) \) looks exactly like the law of cosines for a triangle with sides \( A_1 \) and \( A_2 \) and included angle \( \pi - \Delta\phi \). This isn't a coincidence — it's the phasor addition you'll see in the next explorer. Set A1 = A2 = 1 and sweep Δφ slowly: the amplitude goes from 2 (at 0°) to 0 (at 180°) and back to 2 (at 360°).

\( A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2\cos(\Delta\phi) \). For the cross term to vanish, we need \( \cos(\Delta\phi) = 0 \), which means \( \Delta\phi = 90° \). At 90°, the two waves are "orthogonal" — their energies add like the sides of a right triangle (Pythagoras!).

Phasor Addition

There's a beautiful geometric way to visualise wave addition. Represent each wave \( A\cos(\omega t + \phi) \) as a rotating vector (phasor) of length \( A \), spinning at angular frequency \( \omega \). To add two waves, place the phasors tip-to-tail. The resultant phasor — from the origin to the final tip — gives the amplitude and phase of the sum. The real part (horizontal projection) is the physical displacement at any instant.

Interactive: Phasor Diagram
Key Insight

As you drag the time slider, both phasors rotate together (same \( \omega \)), but the angle between them stays fixed at \( \Delta\phi \). That's why the resultant amplitude doesn't change with time — it only depends on the phase difference. The phasor picture makes the law-of-cosines formula obvious: the resultant is the third side of a triangle with sides \( A_1 \) and \( A_2 \) and included angle \( \Delta\phi \).

Explanation
Both phasors rotate at the same rate \( \omega \), so the triangle they form is rigid — it rotates as a whole without changing shape. The resultant's length (amplitude) stays constant; only its angle changes. This is why the sum of two same-frequency cosines is always another cosine at that frequency, with a fixed amplitude.

The Amplitude Challenge

Time to put the formula to work. You're given two amplitudes A1 and A2, and a target resultant amplitude. Your job: calculate the phase difference \( \Delta\phi \) that achieves it. Rearrange the formula: \( \cos(\Delta\phi) = \frac{A^2 - A_1^2 - A_2^2}{2 A_1 A_2} \).

Interactive: Amplitude Challenge
Key Insight

The inverse formula \( \cos(\Delta\phi) = \frac{A^2 - A_1^2 - A_2^2}{2A_1 A_2} \) always has a solution when \( |A_1 - A_2| \le A \le A_1 + A_2 \). Outside this range, no phase difference can produce the target amplitude. Notice that this range is exactly the triangle inequality — the resultant is the third side of the phasor triangle.

If A1 = 3 and A2 = 1, can you achieve a resultant amplitude of A = 0?
Explanation
Complete cancellation requires \( A_1 = A_2 \). When the amplitudes are unequal, the best destructive interference can do is \( A = |A_1 - A_2| = |3 - 1| = 2 \). The stronger wave always "survives." This is why perfect noise cancellation needs precisely matched amplitudes.
Which statements about same-frequency interference are correct? (Select all that apply)
Explanation
  • False: The resultant is always at the same frequency \( \omega \). Phase difference controls the amplitude, not the frequency.
  • True: The amplitude formula \( A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1 A_2 \cos(\Delta\phi) \) depends only on these three quantities.
  • True: Interference doesn't create or destroy energy. Where waves add constructively, they're brighter; where they cancel, the energy goes elsewhere. The total energy is always the sum of the individual energies.
  • False: Three waves of equal amplitude spaced 120° apart cancel perfectly (\( 1 + e^{i2\pi/3} + e^{i4\pi/3} = 0 \)). This is the basis of three-phase power systems.

Python Code

Here's the Python equivalent for interference calculations and phasor diagrams.

Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

t = np.linspace(0, 8*np.pi, 1000)
omega = 1.0
A1, A2 = 1.0, 1.0

# Sweep phase difference
for dphi_deg in [0, 60, 90, 120, 180]:
    dphi = np.radians(dphi_deg)
    x1 = A1 * np.cos(omega*t)
    x2 = A2 * np.cos(omega*t + dphi)
    A_res = np.sqrt(A1**2 + A2**2 + 2*A1*A2*np.cos(dphi))
    print(f"Δφ = {dphi_deg:>3d}° → A = {A_res:.3f}")

# Plot A vs Δφ
dphi_arr = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 360)
A_arr = np.sqrt(A1**2 + A2**2 + 2*A1*A2*np.cos(dphi_arr))

fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=np.degrees(dphi_arr), y=A_arr,
    line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=2.5), name='A(Δφ)'))
fig.add_hline(y=A1+A2, line=dict(color='#00ff9f', dash='dash'), annotation_text='Constructive')
fig.add_hline(y=abs(A1-A2), line=dict(color='#ff6b6b', dash='dash'), annotation_text='Destructive')
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='Δφ (degrees)', dtick=45),
    yaxis=dict(title='Resultant Amplitude'))
fig.show()
Python 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

A1, A2 = 1.0, 1.0
dphi = np.radians(90)  # 90° phase difference

# Phasor 1: starts at origin
p1_end = np.array([A1, 0])  # at t=0, angle = 0

# Phasor 2: starts at tip of phasor 1
p2_dir = np.array([np.cos(dphi), np.sin(dphi)])
p2_end = p1_end + A2 * p2_dir

# Resultant
A_res = np.sqrt(A1**2 + A2**2 + 2*A1*A2*np.cos(dphi))
phi_res = np.arctan2(p2_end[1], p2_end[0])

fig = go.Figure()
# Phasor 1
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0, p1_end[0]], y=[0, p1_end[1]],
    mode='lines+markers', line=dict(color='#00f3ff', width=3),
    marker=dict(size=[0, 10]), name=f'A₁ = {A1}'))
# Phasor 2
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[p1_end[0], p2_end[0]], y=[p1_end[1], p2_end[1]],
    mode='lines+markers', line=dict(color='#ff00ff', width=3),
    marker=dict(size=[0, 10]), name=f'A₂ = {A2}'))
# Resultant
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0, p2_end[0]], y=[0, p2_end[1]],
    mode='lines+markers', line=dict(color='#ffff00', width=3),
    marker=dict(size=[0, 12]), name=f'A = {A_res:.2f}'))

rmax = (A1+A2)*1.3
fig.update_layout(template='plotly_dark',
    xaxis=dict(title='Re', range=[-rmax, rmax], scaleanchor='y'),
    yaxis=dict(title='Im', range=[-rmax, rmax]),
    title=f'Phasor Addition: Δφ = {np.degrees(dphi):.0f}°')
fig.show()
